The ideas of the French Revolution travelled the world faster than French armies. They were first put to use exactly where that was least expected: on the sugar plantations of the Caribbean.

## Haiti

The French colony of Saint-Domingue was the most profitable holding on the planet, producing a large share of the world's sugar and coffee. It ran on the labour of half a million enslaved people set against a few tens of thousands of free ones.

In 1791 a revolt began there. The proclamation of the rights of man in Paris sounded strange to people who watched those rights violated daily in the most open form imaginable.

**Toussaint Louverture**, born in 1743 and dead in 1803, himself a former slave who had been freed, turned out to be an exceptional commander and politician. He built an army out of the rebels, defeated in turn the Spanish and British forces trying to exploit the chaos, and secured the abolition of slavery.

Napoleon sent forty thousand troops to the island to restore the colony and slavery. Toussaint was seized by trickery and taken to France, where he died in prison. But the army did not settle the matter: yellow fever and resistance destroyed the expedition.

In 1804 the independence of Haiti was proclaimed. It is the only slave revolt in history that ended in the creation of a state.

The price was heavy. Neighbours, including the United States, where slavery continued, refused for decades to recognise the republic. In 1825 France demanded compensation for lost property — that is, for the people who had freed themselves — under threat of war. Haiti paid that debt for more than a century, and it lies at the base of the country's poverty.

## Bolívar

**Simón Bolívar** was born in 1783 and died in 1830. He came from a wealthy Creole family in Caracas; Creole meant descendants of Spaniards born in the Americas.

That was the knot of the Spanish colonies. Wealth and land belonged to the Creoles, while Madrid reserved the highest offices for men born in Spain. When Napoleon deposed the Spanish king in 1808, the question of who exactly they were obeying hung in the air, and the colonies began declaring self-government.

The war ran fifteen years with mixed fortunes; Bolívar twice lost everything and went into exile. The turning point was his army's crossing of the Andes in 1819: he led his troops over the passes in the rainy season, losing some of them, and emerged behind the Spanish, who had considered the route impossible.

Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Panama, Peru and Bolivia, named after him, were freed. At the same time San Martín came up from the south, freeing Argentina and Chile; Brazil separated from Portugal peacefully by declaring an empire of its own.

Bolívar dreamed of one great union of Spanish-speaking America. It did not happen: distance, mountains, local interests and the ambitions of commanders pulled the new countries apart. He died at forty-seven, ill and disillusioned, having written the bitter line that whoever serves a revolution ploughs the sea.

**Why this matters today.** In thirty years the map of the Americas south of the United States changed completely, and the borders drawn then along old colonial districts still stand. And Haiti's history shows how long the punishment for an inconvenient victory can last: the indemnity imposed in 1825 is still felt in that country.
