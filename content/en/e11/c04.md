By the end of the nineteenth century industrial Europe held an advantage nobody in history had held before. Steamships, railways, the telegraph, rapid-firing weapons and quinine against malaria allowed action in places that had previously been unreachable.

Two responses to that advantage stand out especially clearly.

## Japan: catching up in one generation

In 1853 an American squadron forced closed Japan to open its ports under the threat of guns. The treaties that followed were unequal: foreigners set the tariffs, and their citizens were not tried in Japanese courts.

The reaction was unlike that in other countries. A section of the samurai decided the shogunate could not defend the country, overthrew it in 1868 and returned formal authority to the emperor. The **Meiji Restoration** is dated 1868 to 1889.

Then came something very rare: the ruling class deliberately abolished itself. The samurai lost their privileges, their right to the sword and their hereditary stipends; the principalities became prefectures. Not because they had been defeated, but because they judged it the condition of the country's survival.

After that, borrowing by list. The army was built on the Prussian model, the navy on the British, the law on the French and German, the schools on the American. Young people were sent abroad to study and foreign specialists were hired at enormous salaries, on condition that they train replacements. Universal primary education came earlier than in many European countries. A constitution was adopted in 1889.

The result shows in the dates: in 1853 a country with no ocean-going fleet, in 1905 a power that defeated Russia on land and at sea. The other side of it: the same machine took Japan thirty years later into war in China and the Pacific.

## Africa: a partition its inhabitants did not attend

The **Scramble for Africa** is dated 1885 to 1914. At the start of that span Europeans held mainly coastlines; by the end only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent.

The key event was the Berlin conference of 1884–1885, where representatives of fourteen powers agreed on the rules of partition. Not one African participant was present.

Borders were drawn along parallels and rivers without knowing or asking who lived where. Peoples were cut in half, hostile communities were bundled into one colony. About half the borders of modern Africa are straight lines drawn then.

The worst case was the Congo Free State, held as the personal property of King Leopold II of Belgium. Rubber quotas were enforced with hostages and by cutting off hands. The scandal raised by British and Swiss journalists and missionaries was one of the first international human rights campaigns and forced Belgium in 1908 to take the colony away from its own king.

Resistance was everywhere. Ethiopia under Menelik II destroyed an Italian army at Adwa in 1896 and stayed independent, the only such case.

**Why this matters today.** Japan showed that adopting someone else's technology without dissolving into their country is possible, but that it requires the ruling layer to agree to lose its privileges. And the borders drawn in Berlin by people who had never been to Africa remain a cause of conflict now: changing them is frightening, because then all of them would have to change.
