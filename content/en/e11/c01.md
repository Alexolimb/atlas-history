For thousands of years everything that moved on land moved by muscle: human, horse, ox. You could not travel faster than a horse, and you could not lift more than ten men could lift. The limit seemed as permanent as the weather.

Over the nineteenth century it vanished. And at almost the same time, so did the certainty that power comes by birth.

## Ten years that changed the vocabulary

The **French Revolution** ran from 1789 to 1799. It began not with an idea but with money: the state had been in debt since Louis XIV, taxes were paid by the poorer, and a failed harvest had raised the price of bread.

After that events moved faster than anyone could follow. The estates of the realm were abolished. The king was tried and executed — not killed by conspirators in his bedroom but tried and executed by sentence, in public, which had not happened before in European history. The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen was adopted, stating that people are born free and equal.

And then the revolution began to devour itself. Those who had abolished the death penalty were executed by their own comrades. In a little over a year of terror, tens of thousands died, most of them not aristocrats but peasants and townspeople. Out of that chaos came a general.

**Napoleon**, born in 1769 and dead in 1821, declared himself emperor — that is, restored precisely what it had all started against. Yet he also carried across Europe a civil code that abolished the privileges of birth, established equality before the law and civil marriage. He did it with bayonets, and half of Europe fought him for fifteen years. The code, meanwhile, remained: it still underlies the law of dozens of countries.

## Coal, steam and a new clock

At the same time the **Industrial Revolution** began in Britain. The power loom, the steam engine, the coke-fired blast furnace — inventions separately, a rupture together.

More than production changed. Time changed. A peasant worked by the sun and the seasons; a factory worker by the whistle, the same in winter and summer. The notions of "working day", "shift" and "day off" appeared, because before they simply had not been needed.

Distance changed. A train ran three times faster than a horse and did not tire. The telegraph carried a message across an ocean in minutes rather than weeks. A world that the Mongols had shrunk to months of travel now shrank to hours.

The price was heavy and fell on the first generation. Twelve-hour days, child labour, cities in coal smoke, life expectancy in industrial districts lower than in the countryside. Trade unions, limits on child labour and the eight-hour day appeared as answers to this — and not quickly.

## Not only Europe

Japan, closed off from the world for centuries, did in 1868 what nobody else managed: it chose to adopt Western technology without surrendering its independence. In thirty years the **Meiji Restoration** turned it from a feudal country into an industrial power.

Meanwhile European states divided nearly the whole of Africa among themselves in a couple of decades — not because they had grown wiser or braver, but because they now had steamships, machine guns and quinine against malaria.

**Why this matters today.** The idea of equality before the law and the idea of the machine appeared in the same century and have travelled together ever since. The first has still not been fully delivered anywhere. The second works so reliably that we have stopped noticing it — and are surprised to discover that it, too, has a bill, and that somebody is paying it.
