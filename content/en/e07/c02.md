The **Crusades** ran from 1095 to 1291. For two hundred years Europeans set out east to win back the Holy Land, and in the end held none of it.

## Why it started at all

The occasion was a request for help. Byzantium had lost a major battle to the Seljuk Turks, lost most of Asia Minor with it, and asked the West for mercenaries.

In 1095 Pope Urban II turned a request for mercenaries into something else. He called not for a detachment but for everyone — knights, peasants, anyone — to go and free Jerusalem, promising remission of sins. The response was enormous, and the reasons were not only religious.

Europe at that time had a surplus of armed men without land: the estate went to the eldest son and the younger ones were left to fight. The church had spent years trying to damp down their constant feuding. A crusade gave these men a purpose, plunder and a justification, and took them far away.

## The First Crusade and what followed

The **First Crusade** ran from 1096 to 1099. It is the only one that fully reached its goal: the knights took Jerusalem in 1099 and carried out a massacre that Christian and Muslim eyewitnesses alike described with horror.

Four crusader states appeared on the reconquered lands along the Levantine coast. They survived on a constant flow of people from Europe and on the disunity of the Muslim rulers.

Once the disunity ended, so did they. **Saladin**, born in 1138 and dead in 1193, united Egypt and Syria, destroyed the crusader army at Hattin in 1187 and took Jerusalem. He did not sack the city and let the inhabitants ransom themselves or leave — conduct that so struck his enemies that Saladin became a hero of European chivalric romance.

After that the crusades ran downhill. The Third, joined by the kings of England and France and the German emperor, did not recover Jerusalem. The Fourth, in 1204, never reached Muslims at all: the Venetians steered it to Constantinople, capital of Christian Byzantium, and the crusaders sacked it, carrying off everything that could be carried. Byzantium never recovered from that blow. In 1291 Acre, the last crusader fortress, fell, and the era ended.

## What actually remained

The military result was nil. The economic and intellectual result was enormous.

The Italian cities that carried and supplied the crusaders grew rich: Venice, Genoa and Pisa acquired trading quarters across the Mediterranean. Their wealth would pay for the Renaissance a century and a half later.

Into Europe came the eastern windmill, sugar, citrus, paper, Arabic numerals, glassmaking, and with them Arabic versions of Aristotle, Euclid and Ptolemy, unread in Latin for centuries. Europe's universities grew in large part out of those books.

Hatred remained too. Pogroms against Jewish communities in German towns as the first crusade gathered, mutual memories of massacre, the sack of Constantinople — scores still presented to one another today, usually in a heavily simplified version.

**Why this matters today.** Two centuries of war changed nobody's borders but changed both sides from within. And another thing: the word "crusade" is used today as a political label by people for whom it means nothing like what happened in the twelfth century. Knowing the actual events is useful if only to stop such labels working.
