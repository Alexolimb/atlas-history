The **Song dynasty** ruled China from 960 to 1279. Militarily it is one of the weakest dynasties: it never held the steppe, lost the north and ended under the Mongols. In every other respect it was probably the most advanced society that existed anywhere before the industrial revolution.

## What was there

Gunpowder. First as an incendiary mixture and rockets, later as a charge. The compass at sea. Printing with movable type, which Bi Sheng devised around 1040, four centuries before Gutenberg.

Paper money: the first state banknotes in the world, because copper coin could no longer carry the turnover. Coal-fired furnaces and iron output that Europe reached only in the eighteenth century. Cities of a million people, night markets, restaurants with menus, fire brigades, hospitals.

Under the Song the examinations for office became genuinely mass: printed books grew cheap and preparation was no longer only for the rich. A class of scholar-officials appeared that shaped China into the twentieth century.

Maritime trade reached India and East Africa. The compass and large junks with watertight compartments made such voyages routine.

## Why this did not become an industrial revolution

Historians still argue about it. The Song had coal, metallurgy, printing, banks, big cities and a literate bureaucracy — a set resembling the preconditions of eighteenth-century England.

One explanation: the empire was destroyed from outside before it could turn. In 1127 the Jurchens took the north and the capital, and the court fled south. In 1279 the Mongols took the south too, and for the first time all of China lay under a foreign dynasty.

Another: the Song had no rival states next door, each in a hurry to outrun the others. European fragmentation, which for centuries looked like a misfortune, turned out to be an engine.

## Angkor

At the same time South-East Asia held the **Khmer Empire**, from 802 to 1431, across what is now Cambodia, Thailand, Laos and southern Vietnam.

Its capital Angkor was, according to archaeologists working from aerial survey, the largest pre-industrial city in the world: the settlement with its outskirts covered about a thousand square kilometres, the footprint of a modern metropolis, with a population probably in the hundreds of thousands.

It ran on water. The Khmers built a system of canals and vast reservoirs that stored the monsoon rains and allowed several rice harvests a year. There is no tropical city on that scale without such a system.

**Angkor Wat**, a temple of the early twelfth century, remains the largest religious structure in the world. It was built as Hindu, later became Buddhist, and appears on the flag of Cambodia.

The empire weakened by the fifteenth century. The reasons resemble the others: failing monsoons, canals silting up that tens of thousands of people had to clear by hand, wars with neighbours. In 1431 Angkor was taken by the Thai and the capital moved. The jungle closed over the city, but local monks never lost it: the European "discovery" of Angkor in the nineteenth century was a discovery for Europe only.

**Why this matters today.** Both the Song and Angkor collapsed not from backwardness but at the peak of their skill. Development is not insurance: the more intricate a society, the more narrow points it has — a reservoir with nobody left to clear it, a border with nobody left to hold it. Progress is not armour.
