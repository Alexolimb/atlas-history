Medieval Africa south of the Sahara is usually skipped in European textbooks. Yet states stood there living on something Europe did not have: gold.

## Salt for gold

Caravan routes crossed the Sahara. From the north came salt, which is vital in the tropics and cannot be produced there. From the south came gold, which West Africa had in quantity.

On that exchange rose the **Ghana Empire**, dated by Wikidata from about 100 to 1200, in what is now Mali and Mauritania rather than modern Ghana. Arab travellers described its capital, the royal guard and the duty levied on every load in both directions.

After its decline, primacy passed to **Mali**, which existed from 1235 to 1670. Mali controlled both the gold workings and the trading cities and became probably the largest supplier of gold to the world of its day: a substantial part of the coin circulating in Europe and the Middle East was struck from African metal.

## The man who crashed the price of gold

**Mansa Musa** was born in 1280 and died in 1337. In 1324 he set out on pilgrimage to Mecca, and that journey entered the history of money.

According to Arab chronicles he travelled with thousands of people and camels loaded with gold. In Cairo he gave gold away generously and without counting. The result: the price of gold in Egypt fell and, by the reports, took about ten years to recover. One man passing through knocked down the market of an entire country.

Word reached Europe. On the Catalan Atlas of 1375 Mansa Musa is drawn holding a gold nugget, and it was from that map that Europeans first learned that somewhere beyond the desert lay a very rich state.

On his return he brought back architects and scholars. Timbuktu became a city of books: copyists worked there and private libraries gathered thousands of manuscripts on law, astronomy and medicine. Some of those manuscripts survive and were rescued by local people during the war in Mali in 2012, smuggled out of the city in crates.

## An empire that was a league

At the same time the middle of Europe held the **Holy Roman Empire**, from 962 to 1806.

The name misleads on all three words, as Voltaire noticed. Roman it was not, being in Germany and Italy; holy only to the degree that it quarrelled constantly with the pope; and an empire in the sense of unified power least of all.

In practice it was a league of hundreds of effectively independent holdings: principalities, bishoprics, free cities, knightly estates. The emperor was chosen by the prince-electors, a handful of senior princes. An emperor could be strong through his hereditary lands but could not give orders to the empire as a whole.

That arrangement was weak in war and remarkably durable in everything else. It lasted eight hundred and forty-four years and fell only to Napoleon in 1806. Because of it, Germany united into one state only in 1871, a thousand years later than France.

**Why this matters today.** Timbuktu and its libraries are worth remembering whenever Africa is described as "without history": the written tradition there is older than many European universities. And the Holy Roman Empire is a rare example of a loose construction of hundreds of members with an elected head lasting for centuries. Today's European Union is built closer to it than to Napoleon's empire.
