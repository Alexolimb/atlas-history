By the year 1000 the world was arranged as several separate universes. Europe knew China by rumour. China knew Europe about as well. Between them lay the steppe, crossed by nomads whom nobody on either side considered important.

In half a century those nomads assembled the largest land empire in human history.

## How it happened at all

The **Mongol Empire** began in 1206, when scattered tribes acknowledged a single leader, and lasted until 1368. At its height it stretched from Korea to Hungary.

To explain this by cruelty alone is to explain nothing: there was no shortage of cruelty before or after. Other things were at work.

The Mongols fought with cavalry that moved three times faster than any infantry and needed no baggage train: a warrior led several spare horses and lived on what they gave. They abolished seniority by birth in the army and appointed commanders for competence — for that era, close to a revolution. They gathered intelligence diligently, sending merchants and questioning prisoners, and often knew more about an enemy than he knew about himself.

And they used other people's skills without asking about their faith or blood. Their siege engines were built by Chinese and Persian engineers. Their taxes were counted by Muslim officials. Buddhists, Nestorian Christians and shamans sat at court at the same time.

The price was terrible. Cities that refused to surrender were destroyed along with their inhabitants — as with the Assyrians, this was a policy of terror rather than an outburst. Some regions of Central Asia and Iran did not return to their former population for centuries.

## A side effect: the world became one

When the whole road from the Black Sea to the Pacific fell under a single authority, it became possible to travel it. A merchant, a monk or an envoy crossed half a continent on the strength of one safe-conduct tablet.

Along that road went more than silk and spices. Books, drawings and habits went too. Gunpowder, paper, printing and the compass — all Chinese — reached Europe in those decades. Marco Polo travelled to China and came back with an account nobody believed but everybody read, including a young Columbus.

And along the same road, in the 1340s, came the **plague** that killed a third of Europe. A connected world works both ways — this was the first time humanity noticed it.

## The world the Mongols found

It is worth remembering whom they conquered. The Chinese **Song Empire**, which lasted from 960 to 1279, was probably the most advanced society on the planet at the time: paper money, printed books, blast furnaces, the ship's compass, cities of a million people, and officials chosen by examination.

In West Africa, in the same centuries, the **Mali Empire** rose, its gold mines supplying half the Mediterranean, and the city of Timbuktu was a centre of learning with libraries of thousands of manuscripts.

Neither was a periphery. In the thirteenth century, the periphery was rather Europe.

**Why this matters today.** For the first time in history, an event at one end of Eurasia began to tell at the other end within months rather than centuries. We call this globalisation and consider it a mark of our own age. Its first version appeared in the thirteenth century, and along with the goods and the books it brought an epidemic.
