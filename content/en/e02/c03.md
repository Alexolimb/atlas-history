While Sumer wrote receipts on clay and Egypt built pyramids, two more stories were running in the east. Both cover more ground than Egypt and Mesopotamia together, and both were long almost unknown — one because it had been forgotten, the other because it was taken for legend.

## A civilisation found by railway builders

The **Indus Valley Civilization** existed from roughly −3000 to −1800 in what is now Pakistan and north-western India. It was found by accident: in the nineteenth century railway builders took brick from an old mound, and the brick turned out to be four thousand years old.

Its cities are laid out in a way that is faintly unnerving. Streets at right angles, blocks to a plan, brick of a standard size across an area the size of Western Europe. Every house has a well, a bathroom and a drain connected to a covered municipal sewer. Europe did not reach that level of urban sanitation again until the nineteenth century.

And yet there is no palace. No royal tomb full of gold. No picture of a battle, and almost no weapons. Who ruled there, or whether anyone did, is unknown.

What they wrote is unknown too. A few thousand short inscriptions survive on seals, but the Indus script has never been read: the texts are too short and there is no second language beside them as there was on the Rosetta Stone. It is the largest unread writing system in history.

The cities emptied around −1800. It does not look like invasion: there are no signs of destruction. More likely the rivers shifted their beds and dried, and the people followed the water.

## Bones for asking the ancestors

The **Shang dynasty** ruled the Yellow River valley from about −1600 to −1046. For a long time it was treated as a pretty invention by later chroniclers — until, at the end of the nineteenth century, it emerged that Chinese pharmacies were selling ground-up "dragon bones", and that the bones carried inscriptions.

They were oracle bones. The Shang king put a question to the ancestors — will the harvest come, should we march, why does this tooth ache — a diviner heated an ox shoulder blade or a turtle shell, read the cracks and wrote the answer beside the question. Sometimes he added later what actually happened.

So Chinese history gained at once both proof of an ancient dynasty and the earliest samples of Chinese writing. The signs on those bones are direct ancestors of today's characters. No other script on Earth has survived from the Bronze Age to modern smartphones.

The Shang stood out in bronze as well. Their sacrificial vessels were cast in sections in clay moulds and surpass in complexity anything being made in the west at the time.

## Different roads

The comparison is instructive. The Indus had plumbing, standards and no kings among the finds; the Shang had kings, an army, human sacrifice and a written memory of themselves.

The second survived. Not because it was better arranged, but because it recorded things: names, years, events. The Indus left no readable words, and its thousand years are silent for us, even though its drains were better.

**Why this matters today.** We know the past exactly as far as it wrote itself down. A whole civilisation of cities built from millions of bricks occupies one page in a textbook, simply because we cannot read its inscriptions. That is worth remembering whenever history looks like a list of everything that happened. History is a list of what survived.
