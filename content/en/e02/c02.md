**Ancient Egypt** holds a record still unbroken. One recognisable state, with one language, one religion and one arrangement of power, lasted about three thousand years. More time separates the first pharaohs from Cleopatra than separates Cleopatra from us.

The reason is the river.

## A river you can set a calendar by

The Nile flooded every year at almost the same date and left a layer of fertile silt on the fields. There was no need to fertilise and no forest to clear. The harvest in the Nile valley was more reliable than anywhere else in the world of the time.

Everything else grew out of that. The flood had to be predicted, so a calendar of 365 days appeared — the one we still use, minus the leap-year correction. Fields had to be marked out again after the water fell, so geometry appeared. The harvest had to be counted and stored, so scribes and taxation appeared.

And a surplus of hands appeared. For three or four months a year, while the water stood on the fields, the peasant was free. The state put him on a building site.

## The pyramids were not built by slaves

The **Great Pyramid** at Giza was raised around −2560. More than two million stone blocks, averaging two and a half tonnes. For forty-five centuries it stayed the tallest structure on Earth; only the cathedral at Lincoln in medieval England passed it.

The Hollywood picture of slaves and overseers is wrong. Beside the pyramids archaeologists excavated the builders' village: bakeries, breweries, an infirmary with healed fractures, graves with honorific inscriptions. These were Egypt's own peasants, working in shifts and receiving bread, beer and care. A slave does not get medical treatment; a new slave is cheaper.

Why build it at all? The pharaoh counted not merely as a king but as the link between people and gods: while he was in place the Nile flooded and the world held together. The pyramid is not a monument to vanity but a machine for preserving that link after death.

## Writing that was read as pictures

Egyptian writing, the hieroglyphs, lasted more than three thousand years and was then forgotten entirely. People stopped being able to read it in the first centuries of our era, and for fifteen hundred years nobody on Earth knew what was written on all those walls.

Chance helped. In 1799, during Napoleon's Egyptian campaign, the Rosetta Stone was found: a slab carrying one decree written three times, in hieroglyphs, in Egyptian cursive and in Greek. Greek was known. Twenty-three years later Jean-François Champollion worked out the other two scripts from it, and Egypt spoke again.

## Three flowerings and three collapses

Egyptian history is divided into the Old, Middle and New Kingdoms — and between them lie the "intermediate periods", when there was no central power at all, the country broke into provinces and went hungry.

In the New Kingdom Egypt went beyond its own borders for the first time and became an empire: the army reached the Euphrates, and the pharaoh Ramesses II signed with the Hittites the first peace treaty in history whose text survives from both sides. A copy hangs in the United Nations building.

**Why this matters today.** Egypt showed that a state rests not on force but on predictability. While the flood came on time and grain lay in the granary, people put up with pharaohs for millennia. As soon as the climate slipped and the harvest failed, the most durable authority in history fell apart within a couple of decades. That rule survived all three thousand years and has not changed since.
