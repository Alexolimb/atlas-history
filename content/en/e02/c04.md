By −1300 the eastern Mediterranean was arranged more intricately than "Bronze Age" usually suggests. Egypt, the Hittites, the Mycenaean Greeks, Babylon, Assyria, the copper mines of Cyprus — all of it traded, corresponded on clay tablets, married its royal houses to each other and made war by rules.

A century later almost nothing of that world was left.

## Palaces without walls

The **Minoan civilization** on Crete lasted from −3300 to −1100. It was found at the start of the twentieth century by the Englishman Arthur Evans, who excavated at Knossos a palace of hundreds of rooms, with staircases several storeys deep, light wells and running water.

The palace was not fortified. No walls, no ditch, no towers — a rarity for the age. Crete was defended by the sea and, it seems, by a fleet.

Minoan frescoes look nothing like Egyptian ones: no pharaoh towering over everyone, no rows of prisoners. Instead dolphins, lilies, women in bright dresses and young people vaulting over a bull. A world that looks oddly light for the Bronze Age.

Their script, Linear A, has not been read either. Linear B has, the script the Mycenaean Greeks used on Crete later. An amateur, the architect Michael Ventris, deciphered it in 1952, and the tablets turned out to be warehouse inventories again: so many sheep, so much oil. Bronze Age palaces were above all storehouses.

## An empire found through other people's letters

The **Hittite kingdom** in Asia Minor existed from −1650 to −1178 and was the third power of that world, level with Egypt and Babylon. It was known only from mentions in the Bible and in Egyptian inscriptions until, early in the twentieth century, the capital Hattusa was excavated with an archive of thousands of tablets.

The Hittites were the first to work iron in quantity. Iron then cost more than gold: the ore is everywhere, but smelting it needs a temperature the Bronze Age could not reliably hold.

It was the Hittites that Ramesses II fought at Kadesh, the largest chariot battle in history, and the Hittites with whom he signed that peace treaty.

## Fifty years that erased it all

Around −1200 begins what historians call the **Bronze Age collapse**. Within half a century, by −1150, this happened: the Hittite kingdom vanished entirely, the Mycenaean palaces burned, Cyprus and the Levantine coast were sacked, and Egypt survived but lost its empire and was never the same again.

Nearly every major city of the region burned. Writing was lost in Greece: the Greeks learned to write again only four hundred years later. Trade routes broke, populations fell several times over.

The cause is still unknown. There are traces of a drought lasting decades, a series of earthquakes, the mysterious "Sea Peoples" of the Egyptian inscriptions, and a theory that the system was simply too tightly linked: every kingdom depended on tin from three seas away, and one snapped thread pulled down the whole net.

**Why this matters today.** This is the first case in history of a collapse not of one country but of an entire connected system, and still the cleanest example of what complexity costs. The more tightly a world is tied to supplies from far away, the more it can be hurt by. Our world is tied thousands of times more tightly than that one.
