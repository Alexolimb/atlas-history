There is a question that sounds naive but actually splits human history in two: how do we know anything about what came before?

Up to a certain point, we do not. People lived, loved, fought and died for tens of thousands of years, and all that remains of them is bone, potsherds and paintings on cave walls. We can guess what they did. We do not know what they thought.

And then, between the Tigris and the Euphrates, something happened after which the past began to speak.

## Bookkeeping that became literature

Writing was not invented by poets. It was invented by storekeepers.

So much grain, so many sheep and so much oil had piled up in the Sumerian cities that remembering who owed what to whom became impossible. At first they counted with clay tokens; then they began pressing marks into damp clay tablets with a sharpened reed. The marks came out wedge-shaped — hence the name, **cuneiform**.

The first thousands of tablets are receipts. "So many measures of barley received from so-and-so." Humanity has never produced duller text.

But writing turned out to have a property nobody had planned for: it remembers longer than a person does. And a few centuries later the same tablets started carrying not barley but stories. That is how the *Epic of Gilgamesh* reached us — the tale of a king who went looking for immortality, failed to find it, and came home to build a wall around his city. It is the first work of world literature, and it is about what people still write about: death, and what is left behind.

## Egypt: a state four thousand years long

To the west, along the Nile, something of an entirely different scale took shape. **Ancient Egypt** is counted from around 4000 BC and ended in 30 BC. That is very nearly four thousand years — twice as long as the time between its end and us.

The reason for that endurance lies in geography. The Nile flooded every year, leaving fertile silt on the fields, and the harvest was predictable. A predictable harvest means stores; stores mean officials, an army, and the ability to keep thousands of people busy for decades with work that feeds nobody: building tombs.

The pyramids were not built by slaves, as was long believed. They were built by farmers during the months when the Nile flooded and there was nothing to do in the fields. They were paid in bread and beer; their settlements, their bakeries and the records of their rations have been found.

## India and China: the same years, a different road

In the Indus valley at the same time there existed a **civilisation** we know least about — which is a pity, because it was the tidiest of them all. Its cities were built to one plan: streets at right angles, bricks of a single standard size across hundreds of kilometres, drains in every house. Its writing has still not been deciphered, so we do not know the name of a single one of its kings, and are not even sure it had any.

Further east, in the Yellow River valley, a society was forming that would leave the first Chinese inscriptions — on oracle bones. A king's question to the gods was written straight onto a bone or a turtle shell, which was then heated until it cracked, and the answer read from the cracks. Sometimes a note was added later saying whether it came true. That is, in effect, the world's first archive of checked predictions.

## What remained

Of everything invented in these centuries, writing is the one thing without which none of the rest would exist. Not the wheel, not the plough, not bronze. It was the written record that turned one generation's experience into the next generation's inheritance.

**Why this matters today.** The Babylonian king **Hammurabi** had his laws carved on a stone pillar in the middle of the city, so that anyone could walk up and read the rules by which he would be judged. The idea that law must be written down and open, rather than living in the head of whoever is in charge, was born right there. We use it every day and almost never think about it.
