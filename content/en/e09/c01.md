A person born in Europe around 1480 saw two things in one lifetime, either of which would have been enough for an era. A whole continent opened up that nobody had suspected. And Christianity, one for a thousand years, split apart.

The two events are more closely connected than they look, and what connects them is the same technology: the printing press.

## A mistake that changed the map

**Columbus**, who died in 1506, remained convinced to the end of his life that he had reached Asia. He was wrong twice over: about where he was, and about the size of the Earth — he thought it about a third smaller than it is. The scholars who argued against him were right. It simply happened that on the way to Asia, for which by their reckoning no amount of supplies would suffice, there was a continent.

What followed is what historians call the Columbian exchange. To Europe went potatoes, maize, tomatoes, cacao, tobacco. To the Americas went horses, cattle, wheat, sugar cane. Italian cooking without tomatoes, Irish without potatoes, Swiss chocolate — all of it is younger than 1492.

And diseases went to the Americas. Smallpox, measles and influenza, against which the Old World had built up at least some resistance over millennia, met a population with none at all. By modern estimates, between eighty and ninety per cent of the native inhabitants of both Americas died within a century. The Spanish conquered the Aztec and Inca empires with small forces not because they were invincible but because they arrived in the wake of an epidemic.

The transatlantic slave route began in the same years: over the following three and a half centuries some twelve million people would be carried across the ocean by force.

## Ninety-five theses

**Martin Luther**, born in 1483 and dead in 1546, was a monk and a lecturer, not a rebel. The objections he posted in 1517 for scholarly debate concerned a quite specific practice: the church was selling documents promising a reduction of suffering after death, and the money was going to a building project in Rome.

The dispute was not the first of its kind. The difference is that previous ones stayed within church walls, while this one spread across Germany in printed sheets within a month. The printing press had been at work for half a century, and Luther became the first person in history whose ideas circulated as a print run.

He translated the Bible into German so that any literate person could read it — and thereby stripped the clergy of its monopoly on mediation. The **Reformation** split Western Europe and led to a century and a half of religious wars, the worst of which, the Thirty Years' War, killed up to a third of the population of the German lands.

## A world that did not notice Europe

The scale is worth holding in mind. In 1526 the **Mughal Empire** arose in India, the richest state on the planet in its day: a quarter of world production was its. The Ottomans stood before Vienna. Ming China was the world's largest economy.

European ships in the sixteenth century were a minor commercial fact for Asia, not a threat. The advantage that would later look natural appeared much later and for other reasons.

**Why this matters today.** Both stories are about what happens when information begins to spread faster than authority can check it. The printing press did in ten years what centuries of preaching could not. We are watching the same mechanics in our own lives, and understanding just as poorly where they lead.
