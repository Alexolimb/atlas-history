While Europe was taking hold of the Americas, the belt from the Balkans to Bengal was held by three Muslim powers. Historians call them the gunpowder empires: all three rose on artillery and firearms that could break steppe cavalry and the strongholds of the nobility.

## The Ottomans under Suleiman

**Suleiman the Magnificent** was born in 1494 and died in 1566. Under him the Ottoman Empire reached its limit: Hungary, Iraq, the North African coast, mastery of the eastern Mediterranean. Twice his armies stood before Vienna.

In Turkey he is called something else: Kanuni, the Lawgiver. He brought the empire's laws into a single code, drawing a line between the sultan's secular law and religious sharia. For a state of dozens of peoples and faiths that mattered more than any campaign.

The architect Sinan worked in the same reign, building hundreds of structures; his mosques still set the skyline of Istanbul. And the court became the place where a Slavic concubine, Roxelana — Hürrem — became the sultan's lawful wife and a participant in politics, which was unheard of at that court.

## Safavid Iran

The **Safavid Empire** existed from 1501 to 1736. Its founder Ismail seized power at fourteen and made Shia Islam the state religion of Iran.

The decision was political as much as religious: it separated Iran from the Sunni Ottomans and Sunni Central Asia, giving the country a footing of its own. Today's division of the Middle East into Shia and Sunni halves follows directly from that choice.

Wars with the Ottomans ran almost continuously for two centuries. Meanwhile Isfahan, the capital of Shah Abbas I, became one of the most beautiful cities in the world: a square the size of several football pitches, arcaded bridges, turquoise domes. A Persian saying of the time held that Isfahan is half the world.

## The Mughals

The **Mughal Empire** existed from 1526 to 1857. It was founded by Babur, a descendant of Timur and, through his mother, of Genghis Khan, who came from Central Asia with a small army and artillery.

Its peak was the reign of Akbar in the second half of the sixteenth century. He ruled a country whose majority was Hindu under a Muslim authority, and chose the one durable road: he abolished the special tax on non-Muslims, took Hindus into the highest offices, staged theological debates at court between representatives of different faiths, Christians and Jains included, and tried to work out what they agreed on.

The economy was enormous. Historians estimate that India then produced about a quarter of world output, comparable to China and more than all of Europe. Indian textiles clothed half the world.

The Taj Mahal, built in the mid-seventeenth century by Akbar's grandson as a tomb for his wife, is the best-known building of this empire.

Then came the unravelling: successors restored the tax on non-Muslims, sank into endless wars in the south, and emptied the treasury. By the eighteenth century the empire had weakened enough for a trading company from London to take its lands piece by piece — the subject of the next era.

**Why this matters today.** In 1600 these three powers between them governed roughly a quarter of humanity and were richer than Europe. Two centuries later two of the three had become objects of European policy and the third a colony. The gap opened not because the East "froze" but because Europe acquired two new sources of strength: resources from overseas and the machine. That is eras ten and eleven.
