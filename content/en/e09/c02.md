The **Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire** ran from 1519 to 1521. **Hernán Cortés**, born in 1485 and dead in 1547, landed in Mexico with about five hundred men, sixteen horses and a few cannon.

Two years later the largest city in the Americas no longer existed.

## How this was even possible

The explanation "steel against stone" is too simple and wrong. The Aztecs fought skilfully, outnumbered the Spanish thousands to one, and in one battle Cortés lost more than half his men and barely escaped alive.

Three things decided it.

First, allies. The Aztecs took tribute and sacrificial captives from neighbouring peoples. The Tlaxcalans and other subject nations joined the Spanish in tens of thousands. The siege of Tenochtitlan was carried out mainly by an indigenous army; the Spanish supplied command, steel and boats on the lake.

Second, disease. Smallpox, brought from Europe, ran ahead of the armies. The people of the Americas had no defence against it: their ancestors had not spent millennia living beside livestock. Mortality in some regions is estimated at half the population and above. By the decisive siege an epidemic was raging in the city and had killed its ruler.

Third, horses and steel after all. Not as a miracle but as an edge in particular fights: cavalry against infantry in open ground, steel armour against obsidian weapons.

## Peru

**Francisco Pizarro**, born in 1478 and dead in 1541, repeated it in the Andes. His force was even smaller, under two hundred men.

He was doubly lucky: the **Inca Empire** had just come out of a war of succession between two brothers, and smallpox had crossed the country ahead of the Spanish and killed the previous ruler.

In 1532 at Cajamarca Pizarro lured the ruler Atahualpa into a parley and seized him. The Inca offered a ransom: a room filled with gold, and twice over with silver. The ransom was gathered and delivered. Atahualpa was executed anyway. The empire ended in 1533.

## What followed

Spain got silver, above all the mine at Potosí in today's Bolivia, where hundreds of thousands of conscripted indigenous workers and transported Africans died underground. That silver flooded Europe, caused a long inflation, paid for Spain's wars — and largely ended up with Dutch and Genoese creditors.

An enormous transfer of plants, animals and diseases between hemispheres began. Europe received the potato, maize, tomatoes and cacao; the Americas received wheat, horses, cattle and sugar cane. Two hundred years later the potato would change European diet more than any law.

And the transatlantic slave trade began: the local population had died of disease and the plantations needed hands. Over the next three and a half centuries about twelve million people would be carried across the ocean by force.

Contemporaries objected to all of this too. The Spanish friar Bartolomé de las Casas, himself a former colonist, spent decades denouncing the destruction of the indigenous peoples and won laws that were barely enforced. The argument about whether some peoples may own others began then, not in the nineteenth century.

**Why this matters today.** Half the languages, borders and religions of the modern Americas follow directly from those two campaigns. And it is worth remembering that the decisive factors were not technical superiority but disease and an alliance with those the old empire had wronged. An empire held together by fear is taken apart from inside at the first opportunity.
