In the same decades two countries at opposite ends of Eurasia answered the question of what to do about the wider world in opposite ways. One spread outward, the other locked its doors.

## The Tsardom of Russia

The **Tsardom of Russia** runs from 1547, when Ivan IV was crowned tsar, to 1721, when Peter I proclaimed an empire.

The first half of Ivan's reign brought reforms: a law code, local self-government, a standing musketeer army, and the capture of Kazan and Astrakhan, which opened the Volga and the road east. The second half brought the oprichnina: mass executions, whole regions ruined, Novgorod sacked, and a long lost war in Livonia. He earned the name Terrible from both directions.

Then came the Time of Troubles: the dynasty died out, pretenders appeared, famine struck, Polish troops sat in the Kremlin. The country nearly ceased to exist as a state. In 1613 an assembly elected Mikhail Romanov tsar, and the dynasty held until 1917.

The main thing happening in those hundred and fifty years was not in Moscow but in the east. Bands of Cossacks and trappers pushed after furs, the "soft gold" that supplied a large share of state revenue. From the Urals to the Pacific took them about sixty years: Yermak entered Siberia in the 1580s, and in 1639 Russians saw the Sea of Okhotsk.

By area this is the fastest expansion of a state in history. It went through thinly populated taiga, along rivers, by forts: a garrison, yasak — a tax in furs from local peoples — then the next fort. There was resistance, and the subjection of the Siberian peoples cost them dearly, but in scale this is a different story from the conquest of the Americas.

## The Japan that closed

Sixteenth-century Japan was at war with itself: dozens of principalities in constant fighting, and in the middle of it Portuguese merchants with guns and Jesuit missionaries who converted hundreds of thousands of Japanese.

**Tokugawa Ieyasu**, born in 1543 and dead in 1616, ended that war with victory at Sekigahara and became shogun, the military ruler beside a powerless emperor. From 1603 the **Edo period** begins, lasting until 1868.

The Tokugawa decided that the main threat was not the neighbours but change. Christianity was banned and stamped out brutally. Foreigners were expelled, with one Dutch trading post left on an artificial islet at Nagasaki under strict watch. Japanese were forbidden to leave the country or to build ships capable of ocean voyages. Lords were required to spend alternate years in the capital and to keep their families there permanently, as hostages.

The result is paradoxical. Two hundred and fifty years without war, which Japan had never known. City populations grew: by the eighteenth century Edo, today's Tokyo, was one of the largest cities in the world. Literacy rose to levels comparable with Europe. Kabuki theatre, woodblock prints and haiku flourished — all the Japanese culture the world would later discover.

And at the same time the country missed the industrial revolution. When American ships with steam engines and guns arrived off the coast in 1853, it turned out there was nothing to answer them with. The response would be the Meiji era, in the eleventh era of this path.

**Why this matters today.** Shutting the world out is possible, and it even brings calm: two and a half centuries of peace is rare in history. But the bill arrives whole and at once when the world knocks anyway. Japan paid quickly and managed to catch up within one generation. Very few have.
