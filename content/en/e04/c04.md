At the eastern edge of Asia a war had been running for two hundred and fifty years. Historians call it exactly that: the Warring States period. Seven states methodically destroyed one another, and it was far from obvious that the result would be one country rather than seven.

The winner was the westernmost, and by its neighbours' account the crudest: the state of Qin.

## Fifteen years that changed everything

In 221 BC the ruler of Qin finished off his last rival and proclaimed himself First Emperor. The **Qin** state would last only until 206 BC — less than a human lifetime. In those years it did enough to leave a permanent mark.

The emperor abolished the old nobility: the country was divided into provinces run by appointed officials rather than hereditary lords. One script was imposed on everyone — until then each state wrote its own way and people could not read each other. Weights, measures and coinage were unified, and even the axle width of carts, so that wagons could run in the same worn ruts across the whole country.

And a wall was built. Not the one shown to tourists — today's is far younger — but it was then that separate defensive ramparts began to be joined into one line against the nomads of the north.

The price was terrible. The building was done by forced labour in which people died by the tens of thousands. Books that argued with the state were burned; scholars who argued too loudly were executed. The First Emperor feared death so much that he searched for a drink of immortality and, by one account, poisoned himself with the mercury his physicians prescribed.

He died, and four years later the state that had seemed unbreakable fell apart in revolts.

## Han: the same thing, gentler

A new winner emerged from the chaos, and in 206 BC the **Han** empire began. With one interruption it would hold until 220 — more than four centuries.

The Han kept the whole Qin machine: one script, one set of measures, officials instead of hereditary lords. But it replaced the foundation underneath. Instead of fear, the teaching of Confucius: authority rests not on punishment but on a ruler behaving worthily while subjects know their place and their duties. Officials began to be selected by examination rather than by birth. That idea — that a post is earned by knowledge, not by one's father — would survive in China into the twentieth century.

Under the Han the Silk Road opened: caravans went west, and Roman matrons wore Chinese silk without the faintest idea where it came from. Paper was invented. Cast iron smelting, the seed drill and the ship's rudder were perfected.

The Chinese still call themselves *Han* and their writing *Han characters*. An empire gave its name to an entire people.

## Two ends of the same age

Compare this with what was happening in the west at the same moment. Rome too was assembling an enormous state, and Rome too moved from republic to empire. Both ran into the same question: how do you govern territory you cannot cross in a month?

The answers differed. Rome staked on law and on an army of citizens. China staked on writing, officials and examinations. The Roman Empire in the west would fall apart for good, and Europe would never again gather into a single state. China would break apart and reassemble many times — and each time reassemble.

**Why this matters today.** A shared script proved sturdier than any wall: China today still speaks dozens of mutually unintelligible languages but writes with the same characters, and what is written in Beijing is read in Guangzhou. And the notion that you become an official by examination rather than by birth was, two thousand years ago, a great deal more revolutionary than it sounds now.
