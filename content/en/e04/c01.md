Picture a country with no capital. None at all. Just a hundred small towns separated by mountains, each one treating itself as a separate state: its own army, its own laws, its own gods. The next town over is a foreign country a day's walk away. That was Greece two and a half thousand years ago.

Out of that fragmentation came something the world had not seen before.

## The town that argued with itself

In most ancient states one man decided everything: the king. In Athens the decisions were made by every free man in the city, gathered on a hill. Not elected representatives — literally everyone who turned up. Thousands of people stood there, listened to the speakers argue, and raised their hands.

They called this arrangement **democracy**, "rule by the people". It was far from perfect: women, foreigners and slaves — most of the population — had no vote. But the idea itself, that a city's decisions belong to the city rather than to one man, turned out to be explosive.

There was a great deal of arguing to do, and the Greeks quickly noticed that the winner is not the loudest but the most convincing. People appeared who taught reasoning. And one man appeared who simply walked around the square asking passers-by uncomfortable questions until they began to doubt their own answers — **Socrates**. In the end they executed him for it.

His student **Plato** wrote those conversations down and added his own. Plato's student **Aristotle** went further: he tried to describe everything — the movement of the stars, the structure of a state, how animals are born, what makes a good tragedy. He was wrong more often than we find comfortable, but he set the habit that mattered: before you explain the world, look at it and write down what you see.

## Sparta and the price of discipline

In the south of the peninsula lay a city with an entirely different idea. In Sparta, boys were taken from their families at seven and raised as soldiers. They were taught little reading, brief speech (our word "laconic" comes from Laconia, Sparta's region) and a great deal of endurance.

The Spartan army was the best in Greece. Spartan culture left almost nothing: no books, no theatre, no philosophy. Two neighbouring cities give us a rare chance to compare what happens when everything is staked on force, and what happens when it is staked on argument.

It ended with Athens and Sparta fighting each other for thirty years. Sparta won, and both were bled white. And then, from the north, came the man neither of them had taken seriously.

## The boy taught by Aristotle

Macedon was considered a backwater. Its king Philip built an army of a new kind and subdued the Greek cities one after another. For his son he hired the best teacher he could find: Aristotle.

The son was **Alexander**. He was born in 356 BC and inherited his father's kingdom, army included, at twenty.

What followed is hard to believe even knowing it is true. In thirteen years Alexander marched from Greece to India. He broke the Persian Empire — the largest state in the world, until then considered unbeatable. He founded dozens of cities, and one of them, Alexandria in Egypt, became the centre of learning for the whole Mediterranean for centuries.

At thirty-two he died of a fever in Babylon. That was 323 BC. He left no heir, and his generals immediately began carving up the empire among themselves.

## What remained

Alexander's empire fell apart almost at once. But what it left behind could no longer be dismantled: from Egypt to Afghanistan, people began reading Greek, building Greek theatres and arguing over Greek ideas. Historians call this period **Hellenism** — "Greek influence".

Two centuries later the Romans arrived. They conquered Greece by force and then surrendered to it culturally almost immediately: learning Greek, copying Greek statues, hiring Greeks to teach their children.

**Why this matters today.** The words democracy, politics, philosophy, history, mathematics, theatre and museum are all Greek. We still argue about what a just state looks like using the same words they used on that Athenian hill. And Aristotle's habit — look first, explain second — eventually turned into science.
