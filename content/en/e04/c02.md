Rome began as an ordinary town in central Italy — not the largest, and certainly not the richest. Tradition puts its founding in 753 BC. Seven centuries later that town owned the entire Mediterranean coastline, and the Romans called the sea itself simply *Mare Nostrum*, "our sea".

How that happened is still argued over. But one part of the answer is in plain sight, and it is not about the army.

## A state built so that nobody could become king

The Romans expelled their kings early and remembered the experience for a long time. "King" became close to an insult: accusing a man of aiming at kingship was enough to finish him.

In its place they built a system in which power was deliberately torn into pieces. Every year they elected **two** consuls — two chiefs with equal rights, so that each could stop the other. Experience and money sat with the senate, an assembly of former officials. Ordinary citizens had their own defenders, the tribunes, who could cancel a decision with a single word.

The arrangement was awkward, slow and tangled. But in it, nobody could seize everything. The Romans called such a state a **republic**, from *res publica*, "the public thing".

## The victory that broke everything

For three centuries Rome fought its neighbours and almost always won. Its great rival was Carthage, a trading city in North Africa that owned the western Mediterranean. Three wars against it stretched over more than a century and ended with Carthage wiped off the map.

And then an unpleasant fact emerged. A state designed for one city cannot cope with an empire.

Conquered lands needed governors — and governors came home fabulously rich. Armies had to be kept far from home for years — and soldiers began to regard their commander, who paid them and shared the loot, as their real authority rather than the senate. Land in Italy was bought up by the wealthy, farmers were ruined and drifted into the city, where their votes were purchased.

The Republic turned into a battlefield between generals, each with a private army. The question was no longer whether liberty would survive, but who would win.

## Caesar

**Julius Caesar** was born in 100 BC. He was a brilliant general, an even better politician, and a writer besides: his notes on the war in Gaul are still read in the original by anyone learning Latin.

When the senate ordered him to disband his army, he instead led his legions across the Rubicon, a small river beyond which entering with troops was forbidden. Civil war followed. Caesar won it and took power no Roman had held before: a lifetime dictatorship.

Those who still believed the Republic could be saved conspired against him. In 44 BC Caesar was stabbed to death in the senate itself.

It did not save the Republic. After one more war, power went to his adopted son Octavian — who proved cleverer. He did not call himself king. He kept the senate, the consuls and all the old names, gathered every real power into his own hands, and took the modest title *princeps*, "first man". Historians date the **Roman Empire** from him; Ancient Rome as a state would last until 476.

**Cicero**, the orator born in 106 BC who defended the Republic with speeches to the last, did not survive these events. His speeches remained the standard textbook of persuasion for two thousand years.

**Why this matters today.** The idea that power must be split so that one part restrains another is Roman. Almost every modern state rests on it. And the Roman lesson still stands: an arrangement that works beautifully at small scale may not survive being scaled up. Senate, republic, dictator, candidate, capitol — we still borrow the words from them.
