While Alexander was fighting in the west, at the far end of his road lay a country the Greeks knew mostly by rumour. He reached the river Indus, stood there, and turned back: his army refused to go further. A few years later, in 323 BC, he died, and his governors in India were left to themselves.

A man named Chandragupta took his chance.

## How an empire is assembled

India at that time was dozens of kingdoms and republics speaking different languages. Chandragupta started small, pushed out the Greek governors, overthrew the dynasty ruling the Ganges valley, and by 322 BC founded the state historians call the **Mauryan Empire**. It would last until 185 BC and become the first state to unite almost the whole subcontinent.

Beside him stood an adviser named Kautilya, credited with the *Arthashastra* — a manual of statecraft, strikingly cold and practical. It sets out in detail how to collect taxes, run intelligence, conduct negotiations and when breaking a treaty pays. Europe would not get a book of the same temperature for another eighteen centuries, until Machiavelli wrote *The Prince*.

## The king who changed his mind

Chandragupta's grandson **Ashoka** inherited a vast realm and went on expanding it. One unconquered kingdom remained in the east: Kalinga. The war for it was appalling even by ancient standards, with hundreds of thousands killed or carried off into slavery.

Ashoka won. And then something almost without parallel in history happened: the victor announced that it had been a mistake.

He ordered that admission carved onto rocks and stone pillars across the country, for everyone to read, himself included. In those inscriptions the king says he repents of what he did, that true conquest is the conquest of hearts, and that from now on he intends to rule not by force but by what he called *dhamma*: right conduct.

What he did next looks strange for an ancient king. He built hospitals, for animals as well as people. He planted trees along the roads and dug wells for travellers. He banned many sacrifices. He sent envoys preaching Buddhism to Egypt, Syria, Greece and Ceylon. It is thanks to Ashoka that Buddhism grew from a local teaching into a world religion.

Some of his pillars still stand. The capital of one of them — four lions looking to the four directions — is today the state emblem of India, and the wheel from those pillars appears on its flag.

## What came after

The Mauryan Empire outlived Ashoka by less than half a century. A realm that size rested on the ruler's own person and on an expensive apparatus of officials; his successors could sustain neither.

But the comparison is worth noticing. In those same decades, in the west, Rome was fighting Carthage and learning to be an empire through war. In India a king who had won his war publicly called the victory a mistake and tried to build power on a different foundation. Both experiments failed in their own way, and both stayed in human memory.

**Why this matters today.** The idea that a victor's strength does not make him right, and that power can be measured by something other than conquest, is very old — and it was written in stone more than two centuries before our era. The flag of India, one of the largest countries in the world, still recalls it every day.
