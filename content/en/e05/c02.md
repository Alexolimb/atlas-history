School courses usually leave Rome standing alone, as if it had no equal. It had one. For nearly seven hundred years Rome's eastern border ran up against Iran, and in all those centuries Rome never shifted it.

## An empire of horse archers

The **Parthian Empire** existed from −247 to 224. It was founded by nomads who came from the north-east and picked up Iran after Alexander's inheritance fell apart.

The Parthians devised a way of fighting that Roman infantry was not ready for. Their horse archers did not form a line and did not close chest to chest. They circled, showered the enemy with arrows and rode off, and while withdrawing they turned in the saddle and shot backwards — a move still called the Parthian shot. In European languages the phrase now means a barb thrown while leaving.

At Carrhae in 53 BCE the Parthians destroyed the army of Crassus, one of the three most powerful men in Rome. About twenty thousand Romans died and the legionary standards were lost. For Rome it was a catastrophe on the scale of a defeat by Hannibal.

Parthia rested not on a strong centre but on arrangements between noble houses. That made it durable — beating a Parthian army did not mean taking the country — but it also made it hard to gather force for a real offensive.

## A state with a state religion

In 224 a local ruler from Persis overthrew the last Parthian king, and the **Sasanian Empire** began, standing until 651.

The Sasanians deliberately declared themselves heirs of the ancient Persians of Cyrus and Darius. Rule grew tighter: one king of kings, officials, tax registers, canals, new cities.

The main difference was faith. Zoroastrianism, the old Iranian religion of fire, became the state religion, with a hierarchy of priests, a canon of texts and a picture of the world as a battlefield of good and evil where a person must choose a side. That pairing of one realm with one religion appeared here before it did in Christian Rome, and Rome was probably in part copying its neighbour.

The wars with Rome, and then with Byzantium, came in waves for seven centuries. The border moved back and forth across Mesopotamia and Armenia, cities were taken and lost, prisoners and embassies exchanged. In 260 the Sasanian king Shapur I took the Roman emperor Valerian alive, the only such case in Roman history.

## How it ended

The last war between Byzantium and Iran lasted twenty-six years, from 602 to 628. The Persians reached the walls of Constantinople and took Jerusalem; the Byzantines answered with a campaign deep into Iran and won.

Both sides came out ruined: treasuries empty, armies gutted, populations worn down by taxes. A few years later Arab armies of a new faith arrived from Arabia. Byzantium lost Syria and Egypt, and the Sasanian Empire disappeared entirely by 651.

The Iranian language and culture did not go anywhere. Iran accepted Islam but did not become Arab; alone among the conquered lands it kept its language and, later, its own branch of Islam.

**Why this matters today.** Two great powers spent seven centuries treating each other as the main threat, bled each other white in a final war, and both lost to a third force they had not taken seriously. It is among the most common plots in history and among the least often learned.
