The **Roman Empire** began in 27 BC and, in the west, ended in 476. Five hundred years — about twenty generations. A person born at its height could not imagine that it would ever disappear: that would have been like imagining the sky disappearing.

It disappeared all the same. But along the way it managed one thing that outlived it by two thousand years.

## A peace bought with iron

The first two centuries of the empire the Romans called *Pax Romana*, "the Roman peace". The word should be taken literally: from Britain to Egypt there were no wars. A merchant could set out from Londinium and reach Alexandria without crossing a single border, paying everywhere in the same coin.

It rested on three things: the army, the roads and the law. The roads were built so well that some are still driven on. The law was written down and argued over so meticulously that Roman law is still taught in law faculties — not as antiquity, but as foundation.

The price was high. The empire was fed by the provinces, the provinces were fed by slave labour, and anyone who tried to object met a legion. The Roman peace was real and was built on violence at the same time — history rarely divides into good and bad without losses.

## The sect that could not be defeated

In the distant frontier province of Judea a teaching arose that at first interested nobody in Rome. Its followers refused to sacrifice to the emperor's genius — not because they were rebels, but because they considered it idolatry. To a Roman official this looked like refusing the oath.

They were persecuted, sometimes savagely, for three centuries. And then something inconvenient for the state emerged: persecution worked badly. The teaching promised what no pagan cult offered — that before death all are equal, and the last may turn out to be first. In an empire where a person's place was fixed by birth, that was said at exactly the right moment.

The turn was made by **Constantine**, born in 272. He ended the persecutions, converted himself, and convened the first ecumenical council so that the bishops might finally agree among themselves on what precisely they believed. He died in 337, and by the end of the century Christianity was the state religion.

In three hundred years a persecuted sect had become the emperor's faith. No other ancient teaching travelled that road.

## An east that did not consider itself a frontier

While Rome fought the Germanic peoples, to the east lay a realm of no smaller size — the **Sasanian Empire**, heir to ancient Persia. Romans and Persians fought each other for four hundred years and neither ever finally won. To the Persians, Rome was not "civilisation" but simply a neighbour with whom relations were complicated.

That is worth remembering when reading any European book about this period: it has a centre, and the centre was chosen by the author. From the other side of the border the map looked different.

## How it ended

The western empire did not collapse in a day. It thinned out: there was no money to pay the army, the borders were held by mercenaries drawn from the very Germanic peoples they fought, and emperors changed faster than coins could be struck. In 476 a Germanic commander simply pensioned off the last western emperor — and almost nobody noticed that an age had ended.

The eastern half, meanwhile, went nowhere and lasted another thousand years. We call it Byzantium; its own inhabitants called themselves Romans to the very end.

**Why this matters today.** The calendar used by the whole world counts its years from an event in that same frontier province. The Latin alphabet, in which half the planet's languages are written, is Roman. And Europe's habit of assuming that "real" history happened in the Mediterranean is not a fact but a point of view, inherited from people who happened to live there.
