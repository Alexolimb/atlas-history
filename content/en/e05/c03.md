The **Gupta Empire** existed from 320 to 550 and covered most of northern India. It is called the classical age of Indian culture, and for once the grand label is earned: almost everything we now take for Indian tradition took its shape then.

## An empire that did not press down

The Guptas ruled differently from the Mauryas five hundred years earlier. The Mauryas built a rigid chain of officials and surveillance. The Guptas left conquered rulers on their thrones, demanding recognition and tribute while barely touching the internal affairs of the regions.

That arrangement is weaker in war but cheaper in peace: no vast apparatus to maintain. Taxes were moderate, trade with Rome, Persia and South-East Asia was brisk, and everything else grew on that prosperity.

The Chinese monk Faxian, who walked across India around 400, left a description: the cities are rich, hospitals for the poor are free, a traveller needs no permit, and he met almost no banditry on the roads. An outsider's testimony is worth more than court inscriptions.

## Zero and place value

The great invention of this period is the one you use daily without noticing.

Indian mathematicians completed the place-value notation of numbers: a digit's value depends on its position, and an empty position gets a sign of its own, **zero**. It sounds simple. Until then the whole world, Greeks and Romans included, did without it and suffered.

Try multiplying MCMXLVII by XXIII on paper. Roman numerals do not allow written calculation at all; you have to lay out counters on a board. The Indian notation put arithmetic within reach of anyone literate, not only a specialist.

From there the digits reached the Arabs — which is why Europe calls them Arabic numerals, though the Arabs called them Indian — and in the thirteenth century came to Europe through Italy. All modern mathematics, accounting and computing rests on that invention.

In the same period Aryabhata calculated pi to four decimal places and wrote that the Earth turns on its axis rather than the sky turning around the Earth, a thousand years before Copernicus.

## Temples, plays and chess

Under the Guptas the Hindu temple took the form that has come down to us. The Mahabharata and the Ramayana were written down in their final shape, epics that India still films and still stages at festivals.

Kalidasa, the poet and playwright of this time, means roughly to Indian literature what Shakespeare means to English. The game of chaturanga appeared then too: four arms of the army on a board of sixty-four squares. By way of Persia and the Arab world it would become chess.

## The end

From the middle of the fifth century the Hephthalite nomads began arriving from the north-west. The Guptas fought them off for several decades, but the wars ate the treasury, governors stopped obeying the centre, and by 550 the empire had broken into independent principalities.

Northern India would stay fragmented for a long time, until the Muslim conquests and later the Mughals.

**Why this matters today.** The most influential thing this empire left is neither a border nor a dynasty but ten signs and a rule for writing them. The Gupta Empire has been gone for fifteen hundred years, and its zero sits inside every phone on the planet. Worth remembering whenever history is measured by the size of what was conquered.
