While Rome was falling apart in Europe, the Americas were running a large history of their own. It developed without horses, without the wheel in everyday use, without iron and without pack animals — and still produced cities, astronomy and writing.

## A city whose name we do not know

In the Valley of Mexico stands **Teotihuacan**. At its height, around 400, estimates give it between one and two hundred thousand inhabitants, one of the largest cities in the world of its day, larger than most in Europe.

It was built to a plan: a broad central avenue more than four kilometres long, two enormous pyramids, districts laid out in regular blocks. People lived not in separate huts but in large multi-family compounds around inner courtyards — closer to apartment buildings than to a village.

And we know almost nothing about it. Not the name of the people, not the language, not the rulers. "Teotihuacan" is the name the Aztecs gave the place many centuries later, arriving at ruins that were already empty; it means roughly "the place where the gods are born". The Aztecs were sure humans had not built it.

Around 550 the city centre was burned, apparently from within, by revolt rather than conquest. The city emptied.

## The Maya: writing, zero and a calendar

**Maya civilization**, as Wikidata dates it, runs from −2000 to 1521, that is, to the Spanish conquest. Its classic flowering fell roughly between 250 and 900, across today's Guatemala, Belize, southern Mexico and Honduras.

The Maya were not one state. They were dozens of rival cities — Tikal, Calakmul, Palenque, Copán — that fought, made alliances and boasted of victories on stone stelae.

Those inscriptions are what makes the Maya remarkable: they had full writing, the only script in pre-Columbian America that recorded speech completely. It was deciphered only in the second half of the twentieth century, and the Maya turned overnight from mute archaeology into a people with named kings, dated battles and court intrigue.

Their calendar counted days from a fixed starting point in an unbroken tally, and their year-cycle was more accurate than the European calendar of the same period. They invented zero independently of India.

Their astronomy was applied: the motion of Venus was computed in order to choose a day for war.

## Why the cities emptied

In the ninth century the southern Maya cities stopped raising stelae one after another, and then emptied. This is not the death of a people: the Maya live in the same places today, several million of them, and their languages are alive.

The causes, judging by excavation, piled up: several long droughts, soils exhausted by a growing population, incessant warfare between cities and, probably, a loss of trust in rulers whose chief duty was to negotiate rain with the gods.

The northern cities, such as Chichen Itza, lasted longer. By the time the Spanish arrived the classic Maya world no longer existed; what they destroyed was what remained.

**Why this matters today.** This is further proof that "civilisation" does not require a particular list of things. The Maya had no working wheel, no draught animals and no metal tools, yet they had writing, zero and precise astronomy. Our checklist of development describes our own road, not the only possible one.
