In 1750, measured by the size of their economies, the world did not look as it would a century later. China and India produced roughly half of world output. Europe was rising but not yet foremost.

## The Qing empire

The **Qing dynasty** ruled China from 1636, as Wikidata dates it, until 1912. It was founded by the Manchus, a people from the north-east who took Beijing in 1644 when the Ming collapsed under a peasant revolt.

The conquerors were a small minority, a few per cent of the population. They held on by adopting Chinese administration wholesale: the same examinations, the same officials, the same Confucian rules. At the same time they demanded outward marks of submission: men were required to shave the forehead and wear the queue, and refusal was punished by death.

The eighteenth century was the peak. The empire annexed Tibet, Xinjiang and Mongolia, setting the borders China still refers to. The population grew from roughly one hundred and fifty million to three hundred million, largely thanks to American crops, potatoes and maize, which grow where rice will not.

Foreign trade was confined to one port, Guangzhou, and ran on China's terms. Europeans bought tea, silk and porcelain and could sell almost nothing in return: the Chinese market had no need of European goods. They paid in silver, and silver flowed into China for decades.

The British found a way out through opium grown in India. By the 1830s the silver flow had reversed and China had millions of addicts. The government's attempt to ban the trade led to a war China lost. From there begins the century of humiliation — but that belongs to the eleventh era.

## A company with an army

The **East India Company** was founded in 1600 and dissolved in 1874. It was a private firm with shareholders and dividends, granted a monopoly on trade with Asia by the English crown.

What followed was outside any theory. The company acquired its own forts, fleet and army — by the nineteenth century some two hundred thousand soldiers, twice the size of the British royal army. It minted coin, collected taxes, held courts and waged wars.

The turn came in 1757 at Plassey in Bengal, where a company force beat the governor's army, largely by bribing his commander. The company won the right to collect taxes in Bengal, one of the richest regions in the world, and turned from trader into ruler.

The rule was catastrophic. The Bengal famine of 1770 carried off, by contemporary estimates, about a third of the region's population, while tax collection continued. Indian weaving, which had clothed half the world for centuries, was pushed out by British factory cloth imported duty-free, while Indian cloth entering Britain paid duty.

It ended with the revolt of 1857. After its suppression the British parliament decided India could no longer be governed through a joint-stock company and transferred authority directly to the crown.

**Why this matters today.** India was conquered not by a country but by a company with a board of directors, answerable to shareholders, holding the powers of a state for a century. The question of where a private corporation's authority ends and a society's begins was not raised in our time. It simply has not been settled yet.
