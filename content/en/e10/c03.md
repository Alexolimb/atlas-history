The **Scientific Revolution** is dated 1543 to 1687, from Copernicus's book to Newton's Principia. A century and a half in which what changed was less the stock of knowledge than the way of getting it.

## What came before

Knowledge used to be extracted from books. There was an authority — Aristotle, Galen, Ptolemy — and the scholar's job was to interpret him correctly and reconcile him with the other authorities.

The method is not senseless: it preserved ancient science across a thousand years. But it has a limit. If Aristotle wrote that a heavy body falls faster than a light one, you were expected to argue with quotations rather than with two balls dropped from a tower.

## Four people and one method

**Nicolaus Copernicus**, born in 1473 and dead in 1543, put the Sun at the centre instead of the Earth. His book was printed in the year of his death. His system was no more accurate than the old one, because he kept the orbits circular, but it moved the question.

**Galileo Galilei**, born in 1564 and dead in 1642, did two things. He turned a telescope on the sky and saw mountains on the Moon, moons around Jupiter and the phases of Venus, none of which could exist in the old picture of the world. And he began to measure: rolling balls down an inclined board, timing them and deriving a law from numbers rather than from reasoning.

For defending the Copernican system the Inquisition tried him, made him recant and left him under house arrest until his death.

Johannes Kepler, working from Tycho Brahe's observations, found that the orbits are not circles but ellipses. The data required it, and he sided with the data against his own conviction that the circle is more perfect.

**Isaac Newton**, born in 1642 and dead in 1727, put it all together. In the Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy of 1687 he showed that a falling apple and the motion of the Moon are one phenomenon described by one formula. For the first time heaven and earth obeyed the same laws.

## The real invention

More important than any of these discoveries is the method. Observation, an experiment that can be repeated, a number instead of a qualitative description, a hypothesis that can be refuted.

And one more thing, less visible: publication. In the 1660s scientific societies and the first journals appeared. A discovery stopped being an alchemist's private secret and became a report that strangers were obliged to check. Checkability by other hands is exactly what separates science from learnedness.

The difference shows in the numbers: in the fifteen centuries before Galileo physics moved very little; in the two hundred years after Newton came the steam engine, electricity, the chemistry of the elements and the theory of evolution.

**Why this matters today.** What is worth taking from this era is not a list of laws but the habit of asking: how was this checked, and who can check it again? That is the only defence against attractive claims, of which more now arrive in a day than reached Newton in a year.
