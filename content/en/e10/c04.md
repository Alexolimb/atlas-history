The **American Revolution** is dated 1775 to 1783. It began not with ideas of liberty but with accounts.

## What the quarrel was about

Britain had won a long war against France, partly over American land, and was left with an enormous debt. London's logic was simple: the colonies had been defended, so let the colonies pay.

Duties and taxes were introduced: on sugar, on printed documents, on tea. The sums were not ruinous. The objection was not about money but about procedure: not one representative of the colonies sat in the British parliament. "No taxation without representation" became the substance of the dispute.

Then came the usual acceleration of conflicts: a crowd dispersed with deaths in Boston, tea thrown into the harbour, punitive laws, and the first shots in Massachusetts in 1775.

## A piece of paper that mattered more than the victory

On 4 July 1776 Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence. Its key sentence holds that all men are created equal and endowed with unalienable rights, among them life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness.

Honesty is required here. Many of the authors and signatories owned slaves, including Thomas Jefferson, who wrote those lines, and George Washington. The rights did not extend to the enslaved, to women or to indigenous peoples. What was declared and what was done diverged from the first day.

But the declaration stayed on the record. For the next two hundred years the fight to abolish slavery, to win the vote for women and to secure civil rights leaned on exactly that sentence: you wrote this yourselves. A promise put in writing works even when nobody intended to keep it in full.

## The war and its outcome

**George Washington**, born in 1732 and dead in 1799, commanded the American army. As a general he was middling and lost more battles than he won, but he managed the essential thing: keeping the army in being as a force that could not be destroyed in one blow.

The outcome was decided by French intervention, France seeing a chance to weaken Britain. The French fleet cut off the British army at Yorktown in 1781, which effectively ended the war. Peace was signed in 1783.

The irony is that the cost of that help finished off French finances. Six years later France would begin its own revolution, far bloodier.

## The constitution

In 1787 delegates of the states wrote the constitution that is still in force, the oldest one working. It carried through, for the first time consistently, the idea of separated powers: legislative, executive and judicial branches arranged so as to stop each other concentrating all authority.

The second important act was Washington's own. He declined a third term and went home, though he could have stayed for life. That refusal created a tradition of peaceful transfer of power that monarchies did not have.

**Why this matters today.** This is the first case of a state being described on paper and then built to the description. And the first large example of a written principle outliving the intentions of those who wrote it: the authors had themselves in mind, and the text ended up being useful to everybody else.
