After a century and a half of religious wars, Europe reached a simple and bleak conclusion: if people kill each other over what they believe, then someone must decide for everyone. So absolutism was born.

## The state is me

**Louis XIV**, born in 1638 and dead in 1715, reigned for seventy-two years — longer than any other European monarch. He built the model everyone else would copy.

The essence of his invention was not cruelty but bureaucracy. The nobility, which had fought kings for power for centuries, was politely rehoused at Versailles and given the role of courtiers: the right to hand the king his shirt became more important than the right to command a regiment. Government passed to officials who owed everything to the king personally and nothing to their birth.

It cost monstrous sums. Versailles, almost unbroken warfare and the upkeep of the court consumed so much that by the end of the reign France was in debts it never escaped. Seventy-four years after Louis's death those debts would bring a revolution.

## An apprentice with an axe

**Peter I**, born in 1672 and dead in 1725, solved the same problem from the other end. His country was vast and nearly landlocked, and its army and economy lagged a century behind Europe's.

He went to learn in person. The Russian tsar spent months working as a carpenter in shipyards in Holland and England, under an assumed name, to understand how ships were built. On his return he rebuilt the army, created a navy, introduced a civil alphabet, opened schools, obliged the nobility to serve and to study, stripped the church of its independence, and built a new capital on a marsh.

The price matched. Building works and wars ran on forced labour, taxes rose many times over, revolts were crushed without mercy. The argument over whether it was worth it continues in Russia to this day, and it is a rare case where a historical question stays a live political one.

## The empire that was bigger than all

Throughout this century, meanwhile, the most populous and probably the richest state in the world remained China. The **Qing** dynasty, which ruled from 1636 to 1912, had by the end of the eighteenth century gathered about a third of the planet's population under it.

European envoys came to Beijing to ask for trade and received a courteous refusal: the empire informed them that it produced everything it needed itself. By the standards of the day this was true. Europe's advantage, which would be obvious in the next century, did not yet exist.

## People who took power apart with words

And in those same decades, in European coffee houses and salons, a conversation was under way that would matter more than any palace. The **Enlightenment** proposed testing the arrangement of society the same way Newton tested the motion of the planets: by reasoning and evidence, not by appeal to tradition.

Out of that conversation came ideas that sound banal today and were prosecutable then. That power exists by the agreement of people, not by the will of heaven. That it must be divided so that one part restrains another. That a person cannot be tried for what they believe. That torture is not a way of learning the truth.

The first state built directly to these drawings was the United States, which declared independence in 1776. The second attempt was the French Revolution of 1789, which would begin with the same words and end quite differently.

**Why this matters today.** The separation of powers, the presumption of innocence, freedom of belief, the ban on torture — none of these is self-evident or a "natural order". They are conclusions reached by particular people in a particular century, after Europe had spent a hundred and fifty years killing itself over differences of ritual.
