The Cold War was a competition in which direct fighting was impossible. Everything else had to compete instead: science, sport, technology, the shop window of living standards. Two of those contests changed the world more than most wars.

## The space race

The **space race** is dated 1957 to 1975. It begins with the Soviet Union's launch of the first artificial satellite in October 1957.

The shock in the United States was enormous: a small sphere beeping on a radio frequency meant the adversary had a rocket that could reach anywhere. The response was the creation of NASA and a sharp injection of money into schools and universities; a whole generation of American engineers grew out of that programme.

**Yuri Gagarin**, born in 1934 and killed in 1968, became the first person in space on 12 April 1961. The flight lasted a hundred and eight minutes, one orbit of the Earth.

Weeks later President Kennedy announced the goal of landing a man on the Moon before the decade was out. It looked like fantasy: at that point the United States had the experience of one fifteen-minute suborbital hop.

In July 1969 Apollo 11 landed on the Moon. The task was solved in eight years by roughly four hundred thousand people. The computer that flew the landing had thousands of times less memory than any phone today.

The race ended in 1975 with a joint flight, when Soviet and American craft docked in orbit and the crews shook hands. After that the expensive lunar programmes were wound up on both sides.

The side effects are everywhere: satellite communication and navigation, weather forecasting from images, solar panels, miniature electronics, materials. And above all the photograph of the whole Earth from space, borderless. The environmental movement began in large part with it.

## The network

The second contest was quieter. In 1969 a computer network began operating that grew into the **internet**. The customer was the American military, and the requirement was communication that keeps working when some of the nodes are destroyed.

The solution mattered more than the requirement. A message is cut into packets, each travels its own route, and they are reassembled at the destination. Such a network has no centre that can be switched off.

For twenty years it was a network for scientists. It was turned into something public by **Tim Berners-Lee**, born in 1955: working at the European nuclear research centre, he devised the World Wide Web in 1989–1991 — pages, links and a browser — and gave the technology away free, without a patent.

That decision by one person is worth remembering: the internet became common property rather than somebody's asset because someone declined the money.

## The wall

In that same year 1989, as the first web page was being written, the **Berlin Wall** fell, having stood since 1961. Two years later the Soviet Union was gone as well.

**Why this matters today.** Both of the era's principal inventions were born of military confrontation and both proved useful for anything but war. And both were made not by lone geniuses but by large teams working on state contracts — a model worth remembering whenever great achievements are explained by the enterprise of individuals.
