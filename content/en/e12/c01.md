In the summer of 1914 Europe was richer, healthier and more literate than at any point in its history. Trains ran on time, the telegraph tied continents together, and travel between countries barely required papers. It was seriously argued that a great war between developed states was simply impossible: trade and money were too closely interwoven.

Within weeks the **First World War** began. It ran from 1914 to 1918.

## A war nobody was ready for

Every side expected to be finished by Christmas. Generals planned elegant cavalry manoeuvres. Instead came something that was not in the textbooks: the machine gun, barbed wire and the trench turned out to be stronger than any attack.

The front stopped and did not move for years. Offensives costing hundreds of thousands of lives shifted the line by a few kilometres. Poison gas, tanks and the aerial bombing of cities were used for the first time. For the first time it was not armies that fought but whole societies: factories, women at the machines, bread rationing.

When it ended, four empires — Russian, German, Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman — had ceased to exist. In place of the Russian Empire there appeared, in 1922, the **Soviet Union**: the first state to declare that it was building a society without private property. It would last until 1991.

Defeated Germany was handed a bill it could not pay. Many said at the time that this was not peace but a truce of about twenty years. They were wrong by a few months.

## The second: the same map, a different nature

The **Second World War** ran from 1939 to 1945, and it was not a continuation of the first but something different in kind.

The first was a war of states over territory and influence. The second became a war over who had the right to exist at all. Nazi Germany built a system for exterminating people by origin, run on an industrial scale. Six million Jews, millions of Poles, Slavs, Roma, disabled people. This was not a by-product of the fighting — it was its purpose, written down in documents and carried out to plan.

About sixty million people died, most of them civilians. The main weight of the war in Europe fell on the Soviet Union, which lost roughly twenty-seven million. Britain was carried through its darkest months by **Winston Churchill**. The war ended with two atomic bombs over Japanese cities — and from that day humanity had, for the first time, the means to destroy itself entirely.

## What was built on the ashes

Out of that experience came several things we still use, often without knowing where they came from.

The United Nations: a place where countries talk instead of shooting. The Nuremberg trials, which for the first time in history put the leaders of a state on trial for what they had done to their own and other peoples — before that, a ruler was held answerable only to God and to history. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, a short text asserting that every person has rights simply by being a person.

None of these inventions works perfectly. All of them exist because humanity had seen the alternative with its own eyes.

**Why this matters today.** The people of 1914 were not stupider than we are. They were educated, bound together by trade, and entirely certain that a great war was impossible. That is perhaps the main thing to carry out of the twentieth century: the feeling that "it cannot happen to us" guarantees nothing. They had it too.
