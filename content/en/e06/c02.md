After the western half of the Roman Empire fell, Germanic kingdoms divided its lands. Most of them did not last two centuries. One proved durable: the kingdom of the Franks, across what is now France, Belgium and western Germany.

## Why these particular people survived

There are two reasons, both dull and both decisive.

First: the Franks did not go far from home. The Goths and Vandals conquered Italy and Africa, cut themselves off from their own land and dissolved into the local population. The Franks expanded outward from the Rhine without losing their footing.

Second: faith. The other Germanic peoples adopted Christianity in a form Rome considered heresy, which kept them foreign to the local population and clergy. The Frankish king Clovis was baptised in the same form as his Gallo-Roman subjects — and gained as an ally the church, the only working administrative network left over from the empire: bishops, monasteries, literate men, archives.

## An emperor who learned to write late in life

**Charlemagne** was born in 748 and died in 814. Across forty-six years of rule he campaigned nearly every year and gathered under his authority France, Belgium, the Netherlands, most of Germany, northern Italy and a slice of Spain — the first unification of Western Europe after Rome.

Taking Saxony took more than thirty years and was done cruelly: baptism under threat of death, mass executions, whole communities deported. That cannot be edited out.

His second achievement mattered more than the conquests. Charlemagne gathered scholars from across Europe at his court and set them to rebuilding education. Schools opened at monasteries, ancient manuscripts were copied, Latin was put back in order and a new legible hand was introduced, Caroline minuscule. The lower-case letters this text is set in descend directly from it.

Most Roman texts we can read at all reached us in copies made during those decades. Charlemagne himself, according to his biographer Einhard, spoke Latin fluently but learned to write late and with difficulty, keeping a tablet under his pillow and practising when sleep would not come.

In 800 the pope crowned him emperor. Formally that meant the Western empire had been restored; in practice it meant a second Christian emperor now stood beside the one in Constantinople, and Byzantium long refused to acknowledge it.

## A partition that grew into countries

The **Carolingian Empire** existed from 800 to 887. It did not outlive its founder by long: among the Franks an inheritance was divided between sons rather than going to the eldest.

In 843 Charlemagne's grandsons split the realm three ways. The western part became France in time, the eastern became Germany, and the middle strip between them, from the Netherlands to Italy, turned into contested ground. It would be fought over for a thousand years, the two world wars included.

**Why this matters today.** The borders three brothers drew in 843 are still visible on the map of Europe, and the quarrel over the middle strip ended only with the European Union, whose first capital, Brussels, sits precisely in the middle of that disputed corridor. And a second thought: Charlemagne's empire fell apart within a generation, while his schools, books and letters are in their twelfth century.
