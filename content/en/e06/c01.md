In school courses Rome falls in 476 and the "dark ages" begin. The trouble is that half the Roman Empire did not know about it.

The eastern half not only survived — it outlived the western one by a thousand years. We call it **Byzantium**, but that word was coined by historians in the sixteenth century. Its own inhabitants called themselves Romans to the last day, and they were quite right: their laws, taxes, army and capital ran on unbroken. The empire fell only in 1453.

## The city that was the centre of the world

For several centuries Constantinople was the largest and richest city in Europe. While a few thousand people lived in Paris, hundreds of thousands lived here. Here stood Hagia Sophia, whose dome seemed to contemporaries to hang from heaven on a chain. Here, under the emperor Justinian, the whole of Roman law was gathered and written down — and it is from that compilation that Europe would later learn it again.

Byzantium did one quite invisible thing for us. In its monasteries, ancient texts were copied by hand: Plato, Aristotle, Homer, Thucydides. In the West, Greek was almost entirely forgotten. Nearly everything we know about the ancient Greeks reached us through Byzantine copyists.

## A new faith that became a world religion in a century

At the beginning of the seventh century a new teaching arose in the Arabian peninsula. Within a hundred years — not three hundred, as with Christianity, but one hundred — it ruled from Spain to India.

The speed is not explained by arms alone. The two great powers of the day, Byzantium and Persia, had just exhausted each other in a twenty-year war. Their provinces were taxed to the limit, and local Christians, whose faith Constantinople considered heretical, had no reason to defend a distant emperor. The new rulers taxed non-believers but did not force conversion — and for many that proved easier than what had gone before.

Under the **Abbasid Caliphate**, which lasted from 750 to 1258, Baghdad became what Constantinople had been. There Greek, Persian and Indian books were translated into Arabic; there worked mathematicians, astronomers and physicians whose work would return to Europe through Spain. The words algebra, algorithm, cipher, alcohol and admiral come to us from there. And the Indian numerals we still use reached Europe by the Arab route — which is why we call them Arabic.

## North and east

Western Europe, meanwhile, was reassembling. The Frankish realm under Charlemagne united most of the continent for the first time since Rome, and at Christmas 800 its ruler took an imperial crown in Rome — a claim that the empire had not died.

At the same time the Vikings set out from Scandinavia. They are known as raiders, but they were also the first to reach Iceland and Greenland, and America five hundred years before Columbus. Their trade routes ran down the rivers to the Black and Caspian Seas, and along those routes formed the state we call **Kievan Rus**.

**Why this matters today.** The "dark ages" were dark only for one corner of the map. In those very decades Baghdad was translating Aristotle, Constantinople was copying Homer, and Tang China was printing the world's first books from blocks. Whenever history looks like decline, it is worth checking whether we are simply looking in the wrong place.
