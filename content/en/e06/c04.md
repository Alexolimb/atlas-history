The early Middle Ages in the European sense mean decline, fragmentation, the village in place of the city. In the east the same centuries ran the other way.

## The Tang empire

The **Tang dynasty** ruled China from 618 to 907. Its capital Chang'an, today's Xi'an, was probably the largest city in the world: about a million people inside the walls and up to two million with the surrounding district. A regular grid of streets, walled wards, a curfew, and a vast market where Persians, Sogdians, Arabs, Indians and Japanese traded.

Tang was an open empire. Goods and people moved along the Silk Road; the capital held temples of Buddhists, Zoroastrians, Manichaeans and Nestorian Christians, and a mosque. A foreigner could sit the state examination and take office.

The examinations deserve their own note. Officials were recruited by tested knowledge rather than birth. The system had been set up earlier, but under the Tang it became the main road to power. A peasant's son who mastered the canon could become a minister. Europe would arrive at the idea of competitive appointment a thousand years later.

Printing from carved wooden blocks appeared in the same period. The earliest securely dated printed book, the Diamond Sutra of 868, is six hundred years older than Gutenberg's press.

Chinese schoolchildren still learn the Tang poets Li Bai and Du Fu by heart, as Europeans learn Shakespeare.

The empire was broken by the revolt of the general An Lushan, which began in 755. The census taken afterwards shows the registered population falling several times over — some died, some simply dropped out of the records of a collapsed administration. The Tang lasted another century and a half but never regained its strength.

## Japan in the Heian period

Japan at this time was deliberately learning from China: sending embassies, copying the writing system, the bureaucracy, the layout of the capital, Buddhism.

The **Heian period** ran from 794 to 1185. The name comes from the new capital Heian-kyō, today's Kyoto, laid out on the model of Chang'an.

Then something curious happened. In the ninth century the embassies to China stopped, and for several centuries Japan was left alone with itself. What had been borrowed began turning into something of its own.

The imperial court lived a closed life in which neither strength nor wealth counted for much, but taste did: composing a poem for the occasion, matching the colours of one's robes to the season, writing a letter beautifully. Men wrote official papers in Chinese, while women wrote in Japanese, in a syllabic script considered the simpler kind of writing.

Because of that, the great books of the era were written by women. Around the year 1000 the lady-in-waiting Murasaki Shikibu wrote The Tale of Genji, a many-volume novel of feeling, intrigue and ageing that is often called the first novel in world literature. Another court lady, Sei Shōnagon, left The Pillow Book, a collection of observations and lists that reads like a modern blog.

While the court occupied itself with poetry, real power passed to the military houses in the provinces. In 1185 they settled their quarrel by war, and authority passed for seven centuries to warrior shoguns. That belongs to the chapter on Tokugawa Japan.

**Why this matters today.** The "dark ages" are a purely European notion, and a contested one even in Europe. In the very years when the west was forgetting how to build in stone, Chang'an was running examinations for office and Kyoto was producing a psychological novel. World history stops making sense if you look at it from a single point.
