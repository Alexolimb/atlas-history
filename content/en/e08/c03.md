Mongol rule in China lasted less than a century. In 1368 rebels drove the Mongols out, and a peasant's son and former monk became emperor. So began the **Ming dynasty**, which ruled until 1644.

## A fleet the world had not seen

**Zheng He** was born in 1371 and died in 1433. A Muslim from Yunnan, taken captive as a boy and raised in court service, he commanded seven maritime expeditions between 1405 and 1433.

The scale is hard to hold in the head. The first voyage had more than sixty large ships and around three hundred support vessels, with almost thirty thousand men. The biggest ships dwarfed the ones Columbus would sail eighty years later with ninety men in three vessels.

The fleets reached India, the Persian Gulf, Arabia and the east coast of Africa. They carried silk and porcelain and brought back spices, curiosities and envoys; from Africa they brought a giraffe, which the court took for a sacred creature.

These were not conquests. The aim was recognition of the emperor's greatness and the enrolment of countries into a tributary system.

And then it stopped. After Zheng He's death the expeditions were wound up, records partly destroyed, and the building of large ships forbidden. The reasons were domestic: the court considered the voyages ruinous, money was needed for the northern frontier against the Mongols and for moving the capital to Beijing, and Confucian officials regarded maritime trade as a dubious business.

Sixty years later the Portuguese would enter that same Indian Ocean, and meet no Chinese fleet there.

## What the Ming left

The Ming rebuilt Beijing with the Forbidden City and gave the Great Wall the shape it has now. What tourists are shown was built mostly then, not under the Qin.

The Ming also produced the Yongle Encyclopedia, a compilation of more than eleven thousand volumes, the largest encyclopedia in the world until the digital age. Printing it in full was too expensive, few copies were made, and only a small percentage has come down to us.

The Ming fell in 1644 to a coincidence of drought, famine, peasant revolt and Manchu invasion.

## Korea: an alphabet designed from above

**Joseon** was the Korean state from 1392 to 1897, one of the longest-lasting dynasties in history.

Its best-known achievement is a script. Until the fifteenth century Koreans wrote with Chinese characters, which do not suit the Korean language and take years to learn. Literacy belonged to officials and the nobility.

In 1443 King Sejong gathered scholars and set them to create writing for their own language. The result was **hangul**: an alphabet in which the shape of a consonant letter mirrors the position of the tongue and lips that make its sound. It was designed deliberately, in one decision, with its logic explained — a rarity in the history of writing.

The nobility resisted: easy writing devalued their advantage. For centuries hangul was treated as a script for women and commoners, and it became official only at the end of the nineteenth century. Today both Koreas write with it, and the day of its proclamation is a public holiday.

**Why this matters today.** At the start of the fifteenth century China had the best fleet on the planet and could have taken the world's sea routes. It turned around and left, for domestic reasons, out of budgetary and court considerations. The Portuguese took the space, and for the next five hundred years the world was arranged from Europe. Sometimes history turns not on a victory but on somebody's decision not to take part.
