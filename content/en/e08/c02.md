The **Ottoman Empire** is counted from 1299 and ends in 1922. Six hundred and twenty years, longer than most present-day states have existed.

It began as a tiny holding on the Byzantine frontier, one of a dozen like it. Why this one survived is answered differently by different historians: a good position on the border, a flow of fighting men, a willingness to take anyone into service regardless of origin.

## An arrangement unlike Europe's

The Ottomans built their army and administration not on a nobility. Key posts were held by men who were legally the sultan's servants, often taken as boys from Christian families in the Balkans, converted and trained. The janissary infantry was raised the same way.

For a family it was a tragedy; for a boy it was the only lift in that world running all the way to the top: grand viziers were the sons of shepherds. A hereditary nobility of the sort that constantly contested a king's power in Europe could not form.

Conquered peoples kept their faith and their own courts in exchange for a tax and obedience. This is not equality, but by fifteenth-century standards it was an arrangement that allowed a patchwork world to be governed without permanent slaughter.

## The man who nearly ended the Ottomans

**Timur**, born in 1336 and dead in 1405, is known in Europe as Tamerlane. He assembled a realm in Central Asia with its capital at Samarkand and campaigned from Delhi to Anatolia.

His campaigns were marked by demonstrative cruelty: towers of skulls after captured cities are mentioned in many sources. Delhi could not recover for decades after his visit.

In 1402 near Ankara Timur destroyed the Ottoman army and took Sultan Bayezid prisoner. The Ottoman state effectively fell apart, with the sultan's sons fighting each other for years. Timur could have finished it off but turned east, intending to march on China, and died on the road. The Ottomans reassembled.

## The year 1453

**Mehmed II** was born in 1432, died in 1481 and came to the throne at twenty-one.

In 1453 he laid siege to Constantinople. By then the city was a shadow of itself: the population had fallen to a few tens of thousands and the empire consisted of little more than its walls. But those walls had held sieges for a thousand years.

Artillery decided it. Mehmed commissioned the Hungarian founder Urban to cast guns of unheard-of size; the largest fired half-tonne balls and needed dozens of oxen to move. After six weeks of bombardment the wall was breached. The **fall of Constantinople** is dated to 1453.

For Byzantium it was the end; for the Ottomans, the turn into a world power. Mehmed moved his capital into the city, styled himself Roman emperor as well, left the patriarch as head of the Christian community and began repopulating the emptied quarters.

In Europe the event was a shock. Greek scholars fled to Italy with manuscripts, and that influx would spur the Renaissance. And the closing of the familiar routes east would push the Portuguese and Spanish to look for a way to India around Africa and across the Atlantic. Columbus was thirty-nine years away.

**Why this matters today.** One military defeat near Ankara nearly erased an empire that then stood for another five hundred years and shaped the fate of the Balkans and the Middle East. History rarely runs straight: it hangs on accidents like Timur turning east.
