By 1500 the planet held three worlds that knew almost nothing of one another. Two were in the Americas and both stood at the height of their power. The third, the European one, had just acquired a tool that would change everything.

## The Aztecs

The **Aztecs** appear in the Valley of Mexico around 1200, late arrivals who began as mercenaries for older cities. They built their capital Tenochtitlan on an island in the middle of a lake, because no better land was left to them.

Two hundred years later it was a city of two hundred thousand, larger than any in Europe at the time except perhaps Istanbul. Causeways, an aqueduct bringing drinking water, floating chinampa gardens yielding several harvests a year, a daily market whose size astonished the Spanish, and compulsory schooling for all boys, commoners included.

And sacrifices. Aztec religion demanded human blood so that the sun would keep crossing the sky. Captives for it were taken in special wars fought not for land but for people. The figures in the sources vary wildly and are probably inflated by the Spanish, but the practice itself cannot be denied.

The empire was not a unified state but a network of tributaries held down by fear. When the Spanish came, neighbouring peoples joined them readily against Tenochtitlan, and that, rather than guns, decided the outcome.

## The Incas

The **Inca Empire** is counted from 1438 and ends in 1533. In under a century it stretched along the Andes for nearly four thousand kilometres, the largest state of pre-Columbian America.

It was built on an entirely different plan from the Aztec one. The Incas built roads: some forty thousand kilometres of paved routes with rope bridges over gorges and storehouses at regular intervals. Chasqui runners carried messages along them in relay at something like two hundred kilometres a day.

The state disposed of labour. A peasant worked his obligation on temple fields, on road construction or in the army, and in return the state was obliged to feed him from those same storehouses when the harvest failed. There was no money and no market in the familiar sense.

And no writing. Accounts were kept on quipu, bundles of knotted cords where colour, knot type and position encoded numbers and possibly words. Some quipu remain undeciphered.

Machu Picchu, built around the same time, is known for stones fitted without mortar so closely that a knife blade will not enter the joint — in a region of regular earthquakes.

## Europe: the printing press

The **Renaissance** is dated roughly 1400 to 1650. Painting is what usually comes to mind: **Leonardo da Vinci**, born in 1452 and dead in 1519, with his anatomical notebooks, machine drawings and habit of not finishing commissions.

But the decisive thing was not art. Around 1450 **Johannes Gutenberg**, born in 1400 and dead in 1468, combined movable metal type, a press and a suitable ink.

The result: before him a book took months to copy and cost as much as a house; fifty years after him several million books had been printed in Europe. Knowledge stopped being scarce. Neither the Reformation nor the scientific revolution is possible without that; both depended on text that could be multiplied and posted quickly.

**Why this matters today.** In 1500 the Aztecs and the Incas were not behind Europe in administration, construction or urban management. Thirty years later both empires were gone. It was not a matter of intelligence or courage but of three circumstances: steel and horses, imported diseases against which the local population had no defence, and the willingness of subject neighbours to help the newcomers. That is the next era.
