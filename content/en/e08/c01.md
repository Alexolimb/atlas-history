In 1346 ships reached European ports with almost nobody alive on board. The **Black Death** that followed ran until 1352 and killed, by various estimates, between a third and a half of Europe's population. It is the greatest catastrophe to befall humanity in recorded time.

People then knew neither its cause nor any cure. Physicians advised burning herbs and not washing, so as not to open the pores. They looked for someone to blame — and found them: pogroms against Jewish quarters swept across Europe, although people there sickened and died exactly as everywhere else.

## Why life afterwards became easier

What follows is rarely discussed, because it sounds uncomfortable.

There were half as many people, and the land and houses remained. Workers were scarce, and for the first time in centuries a peasant could simply walk to a neighbouring landowner who paid more. Wages had to rise. The English parliament passed a law forbidding pay above pre-plague levels — and the law failed, because arithmetic proved stronger.

Serfdom in Western Europe began to fall apart after the plague. Survivors ate better than their parents: there was more meat per person. Towns, which replaced their populations faster by drawing in newcomers, grew stronger relative to the countryside.

The church was shaken too. It promised protection and prayed — and priests died alongside everyone else, often first, because they heard the confessions of the sick. Many parishes were left without clergy and hastily filled with whoever could be found. Faith did not disappear, but trust in the intermediaries between a person and God cracked, and a century and a half later that crack became the Reformation.

Finally, money was freed. Inheritances fell to fewer people. Some townspeople had spare funds for the first time — and part of it went on commissioning paintings, buildings and books. Historians argue about how direct the link is, but the **Renaissance** began in exactly these decades and in exactly those Italian cities the plague passed through earliest.

## The world was not only Europe

While the West was burying its dead, further east rose the power that would become the longest-lived empire of the age. The **Ottoman Empire** arose in 1299 and lasted until 1922 — six hundred and twenty-three years. In 1453 it took Constantinople and closed the thousand-year story of Byzantium.

In Central Asia, Timur assembled the last great steppe realm and built Samarkand. In China, after the Mongols were driven out, the Ming dynasty took hold, and its fleet under Zheng He sailed to the coasts of Africa in ships beside which Columbus's caravels would have looked like boats. And then China ended those voyages and burned the ships, having decided there was nothing outside worth having.

In the Americas at the same time stood the cities of the Aztecs and the Inca, of which Europe had no inkling.

**Why this matters today.** A catastrophe does not make a society better — it simply kills. But it changes the balance of forces, and what looked like the eternal order of life turns out to have been merely a consequence of there being many people and little land. Six centuries later we are again watching an epidemic redraw habits, work and prices, and again learning about it after the fact.
