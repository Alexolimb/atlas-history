If you landed on Earth twenty thousand years ago you would not recognise it. Northern Europe and half of North America lay under a kilometre of ice. The oceans held less water, because so much of it stood in glaciers, and the coasts ran far out beyond today's shoreline. You could walk from Asia to America, and cross to Australia in short hops by boat.

Archaeologists estimate that the whole planet held a few million people. Fewer than live in one large city today.

## Hunters, not savages

The word "primitive" plants a picture of a dull creature with a club. It is wrong. The brain of a person then was no different from ours, and they knew far more about their own world than any of us do.

A Stone Age hunter could tell apart hundreds of plants and knew which healed, which fed and which killed. They read tracks the way we read text. They knew the habits of every animal in their district and could strike from stone a blade sharper than a surgical scalpel — a flaked obsidian edge really is thinner than steel.

And they worked less than we do. Studies of modern hunting and gathering societies find that getting food takes three to five hours a day. The rest is talk, repairing gear, ritual, sleep.

## The first art

More than thirty thousand years ago people began to paint. In caves in France and Spain, deep in, where no daylight reaches, the walls still carry bulls, horses, lions, rhinoceroses. **Lascaux** is the most famous of them.

The paintings are not naive. The artist conveyed movement, used bulges in the rock to make an animal's flank look solid, understood perspective. These are not first clumsy attempts but mature craft, with a school behind it and, it seems, teachers.

Why? Nobody knows for certain. The caves were not dwellings; people went down into them deliberately. Most likely it was tied to ritual: to the hunt, to initiation, to a conversation with a world they took to be alive.

## A temple before the village

In 1994 a mound in southern Turkey was excavated: **Göbekli Tepe**. What came out of it overturned an order everyone had thought obvious.

Stone circles with pillars up to five metres tall and ten tonnes in weight, covered in carving: foxes, boars, snakes, vultures. Age: about eleven thousand years. Which means it was built by hunters and gatherers, thousands of years before farming, before settlements, before metal and before the wheel.

The textbooks said: first farming yields surplus food, then settlements appear, then hands are freed for temples. Göbekli Tepe says the opposite. First people gathered for something shared — and perhaps took up grain in order to feed that building site.

## The ice ends

About twelve thousand years ago the climate warmed. Glaciers retreated, the sea rose by more than a hundred metres and drowned hunting grounds that whole generations had used. The big animals, mammoths and woolly rhinos, disappeared: partly from the warming, partly from the human with a spear.

A world people had spent forty thousand years adapting to ended within a few centuries. The answer was the **Neolithic revolution**, the move to farming, which took nearly six thousand years, from −9000 to −3000, and which the previous chapter was about.

**Why this matters today.** Nine tenths of the time our species has existed, we lived exactly like that: in small groups, with no bosses, no ownership of land, no writing. Everything that feels to us like the natural arrangement of life — cities, states, work by the clock — is younger than ten thousand years. That does not mean it was better before. It means the present order is not human nature but a recent human invention.
