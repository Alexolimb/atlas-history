For two hundred thousand years people lived in much the same way. Small groups followed the herds, gathered what grew, and slept wherever night found them. It was not a miserable existence: a hunter-gatherer worked fewer hours than a modern person, ate a more varied diet, and hardly knew the diseases that come from crowding.

And then, about ten thousand years ago, in several places on the planet independently, the same thing happened. People stopped walking after their food and began growing it.

## A trap we walked into willingly

Farming is usually counted as a step forward. For the individual it was in fact a step back, and we know this from bones.

The skeletons of the first farmers are shorter than those of their hunting ancestors. Their teeth are worse, there are more signs of malnutrition, and more injuries from repetitive heavy work. The diet narrowed: instead of dozens of kinds of game and plants, one or two crops — and a failure of one of them meant hunger.

So why do it? Because land feeds far more mouths per hectare than hunting does. A farming village lived worse but grew faster — and within a few generations simply outnumbered its neighbours. The choice was made not by individuals but by arithmetic.

Along with it came something nomads could never have: a **store**. Grain can be kept. And what can be kept can be taken, divided, counted and taxed. Out of the granary grew everything else — power, inequality, wars over territory and, in the end, the state.

## The temple came before the town

For a long time the order was assumed to be: first farming, then villages, then towns, and only then, once there were surpluses, temples and priests. Logical and convenient.

In the 1990s, in the south-east of present-day Turkey, **Göbekli Tepe** was excavated. It is a set of rings of stone pillars several metres high, with animals and birds carved into them. Each pillar weighs up to ten tonnes. They were raised by people who as yet knew neither farming, nor pottery, nor metal.

The order turned out to be the other way round. First hundreds of people gathered for some reason and spent years moving multi-tonne stones. And only afterwards, around that building site and in order to feed the builders, did sowing begin here.

It seems bread did not bring people together. It seems something else brought them together — and bread was needed because they had already gathered.

## A town without bosses

Another site in the same region, **Çatalhöyük**, existed from roughly 7100 to 5700 BC — almost fifteen hundred years. Up to several thousand people lived there. By the standards of its time, that is a real town.

And in it, no palace was found, no temple, no ruler's storerooms, no rich burials beside poor ones. The houses are alike, packed wall to wall, and were entered through the roof — there were no streets at all. People buried their relatives directly under the floor of the house.

This matters, because it breaks the familiar assumption that a town implies a king. For fifteen centuries thousands of people lived together with, as far as we can tell, neither rulers nor visible inequality. How exactly they arranged it we do not know and probably never will: they could not write.

## What remained

Almost everything we eat today was domesticated in those millennia: wheat and barley in the Near East, rice in China, maize in Mexico, potatoes in the Andes. Humanity has not added a single genuinely new staple since.

The dog, sheep, goat, cow and pig came to us then too. And so did smallpox, measles and influenza: nearly every major human epidemic disease crossed over from the animals we began living beside.

**Why this matters today.** We are used to thinking of history as a chain of improvements. The move to farming shows it can work otherwise: a decision can be winning for the group and losing for every member of it. The people of Göbekli Tepe and Çatalhöyük did not know what they were setting in motion. Making our own large decisions, we know not much more.
