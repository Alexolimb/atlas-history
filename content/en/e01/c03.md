Farming gave people something hunters could never have: a store. Grain sits in a pit for a year and does not spoil. And once there is a store you can stay in one place, and far more people can live beside you than the surrounding hunting would ever feed.

That is how the first settlements the size of a town appeared. One of them has been excavated in full, and it shows what life looked like before any history at all.

## A house with no door

**Çatalhöyük** in central Turkey existed from roughly −7500 to −5700. At its height up to eight thousand people lived there.

It had no streets whatsoever. Mud-brick houses stood packed together, wall against wall, like a honeycomb. You entered through a hole in the roof by a ladder, and the roofs served as streets: people walked over the top of the town.

The houses are nearly identical. No palace, no temple, no storehouse larger than the rest. Not one building of which you could say "the chief lived here". Eight thousand people spent fifteen centuries with no visible authority — which throws doubt on the familiar idea that a large settlement must have a ruler.

The dead were buried under the floor of their own house, beneath the sleeping platforms. Ancestors stayed part of the family in the most literal sense: the family slept on top of them.

## What it changed in people

Settling down rebuilt life completely, and not only for the better.

Food became more monotonous. A hunter ate dozens of species, a farmer mostly grain. The skeletons of the first farmers are shorter, with worse teeth and marks of diseases hunters almost never show. People lived worse, but there were more of them: a settled woman can raise a child every two years, a moving one about every four.

Diseases arrived from animals. Cattle, pigs and chickens now lived beside people, and what they carried crossed over. Measles, smallpox, influenza are the bill for herding, paid across millennia.

And property appeared. A field, a herd, a granary cannot be carried away, but they can be inherited, seized and defended. From that come fences, boundaries, guards and everything that grew out of them.

## The first metal

At about the same time people began working metal. First native copper, simply hammered like stone except that it did not shatter. Then they learned to smelt ore, and then to mix copper with tin.

The alloy came out stronger than either of its parts: **bronze**. The next era starts there, because bronze needs tin and copper, and the two lie in different parts of the earth. Which means long-distance trade, roads, and people to guard them.

## Where it was heading

By −3500 the settlements between the Tigris and the Euphrates had grown together into real cities with temples, canals and storehouses holding grain that was not one family's but everyone's. Somebody had to count that shared grain. Out of that dull task writing would grow, and out of writing everything we know about people at all.

**Why this matters today.** The town is older than the state, older than the king and older than writing. Çatalhöyük proves that thousands of people can live side by side without a chain of command. Rulers appeared not because nothing works without them but because they turned out to be convenient for particular jobs: dividing water, digging a canal, defending the place. It is worth remembering that this is a tool, not a law of nature.
