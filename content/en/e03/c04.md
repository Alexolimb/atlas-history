While Assyria and Persia divided the Near East, three other corners of the world were running histories that European textbooks long passed over in silence. All three set arrangements for their regions that outlived millennia.

## India: hymns nobody wrote down

The **Vedic period** ran from roughly −1500 to −500. The name comes from the Vedas, collections of hymns, formulas and ritual rules.

The strange part is that for centuries the Vedas were not written down at all. They were memorised and passed from teacher to pupil, with built-in checks: the same words were recited forwards, backwards, in pairs, interleaved, so that an error became audible at once. The accuracy is such that modern scholars consider the oral transmission of the Vedas more reliable than many manuscripts.

In the same period society settled into four estates, the varnas: priests, warriors, householders and labourers. The caste system would grow out of them, deciding what a person was born as, died as, married and ate. Caste was formally abolished by the Indian constitution of 1950, but its traces are still visible in daily life.

Towards the end of the period objections piled up. People unhappy with the power of the priests and the endless sacrifices went off into the forests to look for release on their own. Out of that movement came Buddhism and Jainism — the subject of the chapter on Ashoka.

## China: power you can lose

The **Zhou dynasty** ruled from −1046 to −256, longer than any other in Chinese history.

The Zhou overthrew the Shang and needed to explain why this was not an ordinary rebellion. The explanation they produced was used for the next three thousand years: the **Mandate of Heaven**. Heaven grants the right to rule to whoever rules justly, and takes it from whoever ruins the people. Famine, flood and revolt are signs that the mandate is gone.

It is a dangerous idea for rulers. It means power is not inherited by nature but held by conduct, and that a successful rebellion is proof the rebel was right. No other ancient culture placed so explicit a condition on its rulers.

Later the Zhou fell apart into warring principalities, and it was in that chaos — people look for answers when everything is collapsing — that Confucius lived. His answer was neither a god nor a law but an order of relations: the ruler behaves as a ruler, the father as a father, the son as a son. China would live by that thought into the twentieth century.

## America: a civilisation nobody sailed to

The **Olmecs** appeared on the Gulf coast of Mexico around −1400. This is the first known civilisation of the Americas, and what matters is that it developed entirely apart: there was no contact with the Old World.

They left stone heads up to three metres high, cut from basalt that had to be hauled tens of kilometres without the wheel and without draught animals. They left earthen pyramids, a ball game, counting and, it appears, the beginnings of writing and of a calendar.

All of it passed later to the Maya and the Aztecs, roughly as the Greek inheritance passed to Rome. That is why the Olmecs are called the mother culture of Mesoamerica.

**Why this matters today.** Three societies that knew nothing of each other invented, in the same centuries, cities, estates, temples and a story about why some people command others. That is the strongest argument against the idea that civilisation was born once somewhere and spread. Give people grain and time and they will build something similar, each on their own side of an ocean.
