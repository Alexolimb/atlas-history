After the Bronze Age collapse the great kingdoms lay in ruins, and on the coast of what is now Lebanon rose cities that had no use for grand politics. Tyre, Sidon, Byblos. The Greeks called their people **Phoenicians**, from the word for purple, after the dye they extracted from a sea snail and sold for more than gold.

Phoenicia lasted from roughly −2500 to −64. It never once built an empire and does not appear to have wanted one.

## A state shaped like a trade network

The Phoenician cities stayed separate and often competed. Their strength was not conquered space but a route.

They were the first to sail the open sea at night, steering by the Pole Star, which the Greeks called "the Phoenician one". They crossed the whole Mediterranean end to end, went out past Gibraltar, reached Britain for tin and, according to Herodotus, sailed all the way around Africa in three years for an Egyptian pharaoh. Historians argue about whether it happened, but one detail argues for it: the sailors reported that on the return leg the sun stood to their right. Nobody could have invented that. People of the Mediterranean had no way of knowing how the sun behaves in the southern hemisphere.

Along the way they founded not provincial colonies but trading depots. One of them, Carthage on the coast of modern Tunisia, would grow into a power that centuries later nearly broke Rome.

## Twenty-two signs instead of a thousand

The real Phoenician legacy is not in ships.

Before them writing was a craft. Egyptian hieroglyphs and Mesopotamian cuneiform demanded hundreds or thousands of signs; it took years to learn, and only scribes could write, a narrow class attached to temple and palace. Literacy was power.

That does not suit a merchant. He needs to label a cargo quickly, record a debt, send a letter to another city. Out of that everyday need, around −1050, came the **Phoenician script**: twenty-two signs, each one consonant sound.

Twenty-two. An adult can learn them in a week, a child in a month. For the first time in history writing stopped being a profession and became a skill.

From there the alphabet spread like fire. The Greeks added vowel letters, which the Phoenicians did not write. From Greek came the Latin alphabet this text is set in, and the Cyrillic it is also set in. From the Aramaic branch of the same Phoenician script came Hebrew, Arabic and probably the Indian scripts. Phoenician writing itself fell out of use around −150, but nearly every alphabet on Earth is its descendant.

## Why they disappeared

The Phoenician cities submitted in turn to each new empire: Assyria, Babylon, Persia, Alexander, Rome. They lost their independence almost without a fight — a merchant would rather pay tribute than lose a fleet.

Almost no chronicles of their own survive: they wrote on papyrus, and papyrus does not last in the damp climate of that coast. About the people who gave the world writing we know mostly from other people's records.

**Why this matters today.** The most influential invention of the era came not from an imperial capital or a temple but from a merchant's office, from someone tired of hiring a scribe. And it outlasted every empire that took over his homeland in turn. You are reading this as one of its heirs.
