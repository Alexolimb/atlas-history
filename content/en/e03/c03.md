**Ancient Greece** as a single phenomenon is counted from about −1200. Its beginning is bleak: the Mycenaean palaces burned along with the rest of the Bronze Age, writing was forgotten, the population fell, trade stopped.

The next four centuries are called the dark ages. The Greeks of that time did not build in stone, did not write and left almost no objects behind. Yet it was then that what later set them apart took shape.

## Why the country never became one

Mountains cut Greece up. Between neighbouring valleys it is often easier to sail than to walk, and a valley feeds only so many people. There are none of the great rivers around which single kingdoms grew in Egypt and Mesopotamia.

So Greece never became one state. It became hundreds of separate city-states, **poleis**. Athens, Sparta, Corinth, Thebes: each with its own laws, calendar, coinage, patron gods and army.

A polis is not simply a town. It is a community of citizens who treat the state as their shared business rather than a king's property. Even where a tyrant ruled, behind him stood the idea that power is granted, not owned by nature.

## Colonies from Spain to Crimea

By −800 the population had grown and the valleys had not. The answer was colonies.

A city fitted out ships, sent part of its young people overseas, and they founded a new polis — not a subject one but an independent one, tied to home only by custom and memory. That is how Greek cities appeared in southern Italy and Sicily, in southern France at Massalia, today's Marseille, in Africa, and around the Black Sea as far as Crimea and the mouth of the Don.

The alphabet travelled with the ships. The Greeks took Phoenician writing and added what the Phoenicians lacked: letters for vowels. Only with vowels does writing record the speech of any language exactly, instead of prompting someone who already knows the word.

## Homer, money and the first questions

Around the eighth century BCE the Iliad and the Odyssey were written down, stories about a war fought four hundred years earlier. For Greeks these were not merely poems but a shared textbook: people learned to read from them and argued from them about honour and how to live.

Coins arrived from the east at about the same time. Minting began in Lydia in Asia Minor, and the Greeks quickly made money ordinary. The economy stopped being only barter and warehouse accounting.

And at the same time, in Miletus in Asia Minor, a few people asked a question nobody had thought to ask: what is the world made of, without appealing to the gods? Their answers were odd: water, air, the boundless. The answer matters less than the habit of looking for a cause inside the world rather than outside it. Science begins there.

By −500 everything is ready: cities, a fleet, an alphabet, money, poetry and argument. Next come Athens, Sparta, the Persian wars and Alexander — the following era.

**Why this matters today.** Fragmentation is usually counted a weakness. For the Greeks it was an engine: hundreds of cities tried different ways of arranging power — rule by the few, rule by one, rule by the assembly — and compared results. One large empire cannot run that experiment. The word "politics" comes from "polis" and means literally "the business of the city".
