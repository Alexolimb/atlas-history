Iron changed war more than anything else until gunpowder. Bronze needed tin, and tin was rare and expensive in the ancient world — bronze weapons were a privilege. Iron ore is almost everywhere. It became possible to arm not a war band but an army.

The Assyrians understood this first.

## An empire that ruled by terror

**Assyria** assembled the first genuinely large state of the Near East, from the Persian Gulf to Egypt. Its army was professional, with engineers, siege machines and cavalry, and in battle it hardly ever lost.

The conquered were governed simply and terribly. A rebellious city was slaughtered outright and the survivors resettled a thousand kilometres away — so that people were left with no land, no neighbours and no memory of being a people at all. The Assyrian kings did not hide this: they had accounts of the reprisals carved on palace walls, with numbers and details. It was not cruelty for its own sake but policy: word of what happens to the disobedient had to run ahead of the army.

It worked exactly as long as the army was stronger than everyone else. When Assyria weakened at the end of the seventh century BC, its neighbours combined and erased it so thoroughly that two centuries later Greeks passing the ruins of Nineveh no longer knew whose city it had been.

It did leave one good trace. King Ashurbanipal gathered a library in his capital and ordered copies of every text that could be found brought there. It burned with the city — and survived precisely because of that: the fire baked the clay tablets into pottery. Thanks to someone else's destruction, we read Sumerian texts today.

## Persia: a different answer to the same question

Half a century later, on the same lands, the **Achaemenid Empire** arose. It began in 550 BC and lasted until 330 BC — the largest state the world had yet seen.

Its founder **Cyrus**, who died in 530 BC, did the exact opposite. He let the deported peoples go home. He returned their gods to them and gave money to rebuild their temples. He did not require them to change faith or custom, and from the local nobility he required only two things: taxes and quiet.

The calculation was not kindness but arithmetic. A people left with its temple and its language rebels less often, and therefore costs less. An empire that need not garrison every town reaches further.

The Persians built roads across the realm and ran a post with relay horses — a courier covered fifteen hundred kilometres in a week. They introduced one coinage and one set of measures. They divided the country into provinces under governors, each shadowed by a separate general and a separate inspector reporting straight to the king, so that no governor could break away.

Two centuries later Alexander destroyed this empire — but destroyed it as a military force. He left the machinery of it almost untouched, because nobody had thought of anything better.

**Why this matters today.** Two neighbouring experiments run under nearly identical conditions give an unusually clean answer. The empire of terror lasted about three centuries and was wiped out by its own subjects. The empire that left people their language and their gods lasted longer, reached further and outlived even its own conquest — its arrangements passed to the victor as an inheritance.
